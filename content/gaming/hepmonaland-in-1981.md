*Part 2 of four. Part 1, "The Forbidden City Cook Left Behind", establishes what I1 owes to the lost-city tradition and what survives when that debt is paid. This part asks a different question: what did the module put into Hepmonaland, and what was already there?*

*Evidence: I1, both 1981 printings, and the 1980 World of Greyhawk folio. Nothing published after 1981 is used, including to check a conclusion.*

---

## The placement

Cook set the Forbidden City in Hepmonaland, on the World of Greyhawk map, in unexplored and unmapped mountains south of the Pelisso Swamp. The module says so in a single line of its notes for the DM, and that line is the whole of its stated relationship to the setting.

The folio had appeared the year before and gave the region two things. Its account of the migrations has the majority of the Suloise pushed to the extreme south, into the Amedio Jungle, the Tilvanot Peninsula, the Duxchan Islands, and as far as across the narrow Tilva Strait into Hepmonaland. Its entry on the Lordship of the Isles has the Duxchan islanders profiting hugely from cargoes brought from Hepmonaland to the Great Kingdom, and collecting tribute from states wishing to use the strait.

So Hepmonaland had a population and an export trade before I1. What it did not have was an entry of its own, or any description of a society living in it.

## The economy the folio built

The trade is more specific than that summary suggests, though the specifics are not Hepmonaland's.

The Sea Princes are described sending expeditions to the Amedio coasts and trading northwards with rare woods, spices, ivory and gold, wrested from the jungle savages. Their listed resources are rare woods, spices, gold and gems. The Lordship of the Isles lists rare woods and spices.

Note what is and is not established. The folio itemises what comes out of the **Amedio**. For Hepmonaland it says only that cargoes come from there and that others grow rich moving them. The contents are never specified.

What the folio does establish, across both jungles, is a pattern: high-value goods originate in the southern jungles, move north, and enrich people who are not from there. In the Amedio the mechanism is named outright, and it is extraction. Outsiders go in, take from the people living there, and carry the proceeds to northern markets.

## What I1 inherited

The module's premise sits inside that pattern.

Caravans out of the southern jungles have been destroyed. The goods they carried were rare pieces of art, scrolls, books and other items expected to fetch good prices in the kingdoms of the north. Survivors came back with stories of singing snakes, deformed ape-men, men who were not men and horrid flowers, which the merchants discount as fever.

What they do not discount is the commercial fact. None of the goods taken from the caravans has ever appeared in northern markets, and some were distinctive enough to be identifiable. The module draws the conclusion for the reader: someone or something is hoarding.

So the first inheritance is not a cultural register. It is an economic one. The southern jungle already produced valuable goods; those goods already moved north; northern wealth already depended in part on value taken out of lands the folio left undescribed. I1 does not invent that traffic. It gives it a destination and an opposition.

## The reversal

The module makes the yuan-ti the organisers of the caravan raids, assisted by Horan, who has convinced them to rebuild their empire. Its wandering-monster table can generate a pureblood-led caravan leaving the city with stolen goods worth between 5,000 and 10,000 gold pieces, escorted by tasloi carrying bundles, bugbears as guards, bullywugs, and an assistant magic-user from Horan's compound.

The same broad movement persists. Valuable material passes through the southern jungle and is worth money elsewhere. But the direction of control has changed.

In the folio, outsiders enter the southern jungle, take goods from the people living there, and carry them north. In I1, goods travelling north are intercepted by people in the jungle and removed from the market altogether. The city does not export. It accumulates.

The inherited structure is extraction from the south for northern benefit. I1 turns that structure around.

That is a claim about what two published texts do when read together. It is not a claim about intention. Nothing available says Cook meant to invert the folio's political economy, and the module never mentions the folio's trade at all.

## The society I1 supplies

The reversal matters because the place doing it is no longer a blank on the map.

Part 1 established the cultural register of the published module and separated provenance from cultural direction. That distinction still holds here: elements with antecedents remain part of the society I1 contains, even where they cannot be credited to Cook as inventions.

Bamboo builds the bullywug stockade, screens the paths in Horan's garden, arches over his lily pond and is sharpened into pungi stakes at the tunnel mouths. A large brass gong and striker sit under a thatched roof on the bullywug platform. Canoes serve at the sacrificial pool and on the lake. All of these have antecedents in the lost-city material examined in Part 1. None of them is thereby removed from the city the module publishes.

Alongside them sits the household material that survived those controls. Rice in a storeroom and rice paper on a writing platform. Rice-straw matting on floors in three separate places. Brightly painted sliding paper panels for doors. A square stone fire pit sunk in a wood floor beneath a smoke hole, with a kettle on an iron frame. Low tables, silken cushions, a shelf of statuettes, tubs of pickled plums. An inkpot of red ink, two brushes, an inking stone. Scroll paintings. At A2, a statue of a snake-bodied, six-armed woman between charcoal braziers. In the lake, a creature the module itself calls a type of oriental dragon.

I1 therefore does more than place a dungeon in Hepmonaland. It gives part of Hepmonaland a cultural register, which the folio had not done for anywhere in the region.

## Rank, commerce and permission

It also supplies social organisation, and this is the part most easily overlooked because it is stated in the past tense about a ruin.

The city was laid out by rank. Government and important business buildings occupied one quarter. The homes of many important nobles occupied another. The hovels of the poor ran along the northern edge. Warehouses and the shops of tradesmen and merchants filled the rest. Horan's walled compound stands inside the section reserved for nobility.

That is government, rank, commerce, poverty, storage, specialised trades and an urban geography organised around them. Not a collection of exotic furnishings but a working settlement, described as having been one.

Movement through the city is administered. Horan issues gold tablets which his agents show the Main Entrance guards to prove they are on his business, and the guards at C11 will attack anyone attempting to enter or leave without one.

Part 1 used that evidence for a different purpose, because the pass system survives comparison with the lost-city controls and is therefore distinctive. Here its significance is what it shows about the society: authority that can be delegated, represented by a portable object, and recognised by people enforcing access on someone else's behalf.

None of that was in the folio, for Hepmonaland or for anywhere else in the southern jungles.

## The limit

I1 describes one city, and much of that city is held by factions, ruins and later arrivals who did not build it. The module itself gives three incompatible accounts of who the original inhabitants were.

So the evidence does not support turning one settlement's internal arrangement into an ethnography of a continent. What can be said is narrower and still worth saying.

The folio supplied the frame: a southern land reached by Suloise migration, trade moving north through the Duxchan islands, and an economy in which outsiders extracted wealth from southern jungles for northern markets.

The traffic remains; what stands behind it has changed. The blank point on the trade route became a settlement with households, writing, worship, rank, merchants, poor quarters, guarded movement and its own accumulation of goods that never reach the north.

The same south-to-north economy remains visible around it. But the people in the jungle are no longer only the people from whom value is taken.

In I1, they take it back.

That is as far as the 1980 and 1981 evidence allows. It does not establish what Hepmonaland became afterwards, whether this direction was developed or abandoned, or whether anyone involved understood the change in the terms used here. It establishes that when Cook placed the Forbidden City in Hepmonaland he did not write into a wholly empty space, and that what he added was the society on the other side of that trade.

## Questions the text leaves open

*Some could still be answered by David Cook or other contemporary testimony. Others belong to the referee, because the published module does not settle them.*

**For David Cook, or contemporary testimony**

> When you placed the Forbidden City in Hepmonaland, were you consciously working from the 1980 World of Greyhawk folio's Suel migration and Duxchan trade material, or was Hepmonaland mainly a convenient unmapped location for the adventure?

> Did you have any picture of the wider society outside the Forbidden City, or was the city's cultural detail intended to stand on its own?

**For the referee**

> Is the Forbidden City an isolated cultural exception within Hepmonaland, or evidence of a wider society beyond the mapped ruins?

> If caravans are being intercepted rather than trade simply ceasing, who still controls the routes between the city, the southern jungle and the northern markets?

> What does Horan's ability to issue recognised tablets imply about authority outside his own compound?

> If the city once had nobles, merchants, tradesmen and poor quarters, what institutions or descendants of those groups still exist beyond the keyed ruins?
