## 1. The opening act, and a camouflage for it

The module's opening on p.7 is probably the most recognisable opening in the hobby. The party begins in an unnamed tavern in a nameless province, a Rhenari messenger arrives with a sealed letter from the Burgomaster of Barovia pleading for help for Ireena Kolyana, whom it calls "the love of my life", the party marches five hours to the gates (p.7), and three turns [30 minutes] into the woods beyond them they find a dead villager holding a second letter that reveals the first as a forgery. The first letter drew the party in under false pretences. The real letter, arriving too late to act on, asks them not to come. By the time they have the information to decide, the gates have closed behind them.

**The messenger.** I6 gives the clan as neutral evil (pp.6, 11 and 32), with two printed exceptions: Madam Eva, chaotic neutral (p.11), and the three who own the tavern at E2, lawful neutral (p.8). The guide treats the messenger as neutral evil. He knows that he serves Strahd, the "master" he speaks of, that the letter he carries is a forgery and the bait, and that few who enter the valley come out. He does not know what his master intends for the party. Everything he says aloud is literally true: "you will come to my master's aid", "There you will find my master in Barovia" (p.7). The forged letter does the lying. Asked directly who wrote it, he answers truthfully, evades, refuses or lies, and only a lie gives *detect lie* a falsehood to find. *ESP* finds his master and his fear in his thoughts; *know alignment* reads him exactly; a paladin who suspects him, and concentrates on detecting evil within 60 feet while seeking it in the right general direction, will most likely find it, and one who does not look will not (PHB p.22). Charmed, he treats the caster as a trusted friend: he is likely to warn the party off and admit whom he serves, but, fearing Strahd more than the party (p.32), he is unlikely to go back with them. A suspicious party may question him, hold him or follow him. He takes the west road he named, and only the Rhenari pass through Barovia at will (p.32). A ranger who follows him tracks by the PHB's procedure (p.24), and a pursuer the dice keep on his trail is not shaken off by the fog. A party that keeps going has chosen to travel at night against his own warning, and from the gates the valley's procedures, the night register among them, apply (Section 3). These characterisations are the guide's.

This opening works. It has also been read and played often enough that a player who has encountered I6 before will recognise it the moment the boxed text begins. What follows is a camouflaged version of the same opening that reaches the same points in the valley by a different route, offered as an interpretation a DM can fit to any of the published-setting placements or homebrew equivalents set out in Part I Sections 1d and 1e.

### 1a. What the camouflage preserves, and what it changes

The module's opening delivers four things to the party. It places them on the road into Barovia. It gets them through the gates. It gives them the Burgomaster's letter, which names Ireena, names the threat, and points them towards the village. It establishes that the path in is closed behind them. Everything the adventure needs from the opening is in these four deliveries.

The camouflage preserves all four. What it changes is the mechanism of the hook. Instead of a forged letter delivered to the party in a tavern, the hook becomes paid escort work offered by a band of Rhenari musicians who happen to be at the same tavern the party is passing through. The party accompanies the Rhenari up the mountain road, crosses the gates without registering the moment, camps inside the valley with their employers, and, if the plan works, wakes to find them gone and a possession taken with them. The Burgomaster's letter, in this version, is the only letter, and arrives on the road after the crossing rather than before it.

The deception shifts from textual to social: not a forged letter, but an employer who turns out to be their enemy. Both do the same structural work.

### 1b. The hamlet, fitted to the placement

The camouflaged opening requires a place the party can plausibly be the evening before they walk up the mountain road. The module's own inn sits a few hours' march east of the gates (p.7). The camouflage needs the party further out. Part I Sections 1d and 1e place Barovia in three published settings and offer a generic frame for homebrew, and the hamlet the camouflage needs fits any of them without adjustment because it is generic to low-density frontier country.

The hamlet sits a day's walk below the mountain road that climbs to Barovia, inside whichever of the surrounding nations the setting places there. Provincial, largely autonomous, rarely visited by the militia. One farm works the land, split across two sites because the geography requires it, with crops on one and a vineyard and orchard on the sunny slope, supplying the tavern and a weekly market. One or two small shrines. Cottages with gardens. The tavern is the hamlet's gathering hall and the only place a stranger would sleep.

The distance matters. A hamlet a day away puts real distance between the party and the valley. When the party later considers retreat, the hamlet remains a safe point in their mental map, and the knowledge that help is a day's walk they no longer have is part of what the valley costs them.

The hamlet sells the basics any travelling hamlet sells. Food, drink, and beds at the tavern. Eggs, cheese, bread, root vegetables, salted meat, cider from cottage gardens. Rope, candles, oil, and a few arrows from whichever cottage keeps them. The weekly market, when it falls, brings tinkers and pack merchants with a wider selection. What the hamlet does not stock is specialist adventuring gear. A party arriving undergeared leaves the hamlet the way they came.

### 1c. The Rhenari and the offer

The Rhenari at the tavern front as travelling musicians, and the front holds because it is also true. They play, they resupply, they move on. Their commerce in the area is long-standing and the hamlet treats them as familiar outsiders. Nothing in the evening's performance signals what they are underneath the music.

The offer is made through the Rhenari leader, Anshel Rhen, who approaches the party's table after the set in the ordinary way a performer approaches appreciative patrons. His people are leaving in the morning on a leg of their route that crosses the mountain road. The road is dangerous, he says, and they have lost members before. He is willing to pay well for an escort on the crossing. He is evasive about the destination, which reads as ordinary reticence about the patrons on the far side, and he does not name Barovia.

**What Anshel knows and wants (the guide's).** He and his band are neutral evil by default, as the clan is, and the DM may make exceptions among them. He knows that the party is being delivered into Barovia for his master, the plan for the last watch and the item to be taken, and the camp ground and the road; he does not know what Strahd intends for the party. He wants payment and favour, the delivery done, and his people unharmed. Magic and questioning meet him as they meet the messenger, with Barovia in his thoughts; his evasion about the destination is reticence, and asked directly he answers truthfully, evades, refuses or lies. Detection of evil shows that evil is present, not its exact kind. Charmed, he is likely to admit the destination and abandon the theft, and unlikely to betray his band to harm.

The offer is plausible because it is ordinary. Travelling performers hiring escorts for a dangerous stretch is frontier business of a kind the party will have seen before. It works when it is attractive: the pay good, the employer professional, the risk apparently manageable. The Rhenari do not openly reveal their allegiance, but the characters may become suspicious or discover it through their own actions. If they refuse the offer, the camouflage ends without penalty and the DM returns to the module's opening. If they accept knowing what their employers are, the escort goes ahead as a contest between people who both know the truth.

No seer travels with the Rhenari at this point. No fortune-teller, no mystical dressing, no archetype a reading player could map to a Ravenloft adventure. Madam Eva is at Tser Pool, which is where the party will meet her.

### 1d. The crossing the party does not see

The road climbs through the day. Farmland thins to pasture, pasture to foothills, foothills to wooded slopes. The Rhenari travel at a working pace and keep it.

The woods thicken through the afternoon and the fog thickens with them. At no identifiable moment does the country change. The trees get taller and darker, the road more deeply shadowed, the light more grey, the silence more insistent. Somewhere in this stretch the road passes between the Barovia gates. The iron gates open for the Rhenari and close behind the last wagon. The module's gates are not subtle: high stone buttresses, huge iron gates that screech as they open, and two headless statues of armed guardians (p.7). The party will see them. Without context they read as an old frontier gate on a mountain road, and nothing about them yet says the party cannot pass back through.

This is the crossing, and the party does not know it has happened. The fog has been in their lungs since their first breath of the valley's air. The quiet horror of the camouflaged opening lives here. Part I Section 4d's first principle, that Strahd chooses when he attacks, finds its opening move in his servants' work: not in an ambush but in an unnoticed threshold.

The module's dead villager lies in the woods inside the gates (C, p.7). The Rhenari know the body is there and time the party's pace so it stays out of sight. The body will be found tomorrow, on the same stretch, when the party walks it alone.

**The protected crossing (the guide's ruling).** While the party travels and camps with the Rhenari, the valley keeps its secret. From the gates until the protected crossing ends:
- no encounter check is made, by day or by night;
- the woods' worg mechanic does not run for the party;
- Strahd does not know the party has arrived. Only the Rhenari know, and their news reaches him through a messenger sent from their camp at Tser Pool, by the first morning. Until the dawn report his reports do not locate the party, and he makes no harassment, strike or personal attack on it.

The fog works on the party as on anyone: it has breathed the valley's air since the gates, and nothing yet tells the characters so (3a). The protection belongs to the band's company. A character who leaves it while the band is still there, to scout, to wander from the camp or to walk back to look at the gates, is outside it while away: the encounter check runs for that character, and in the woods at C the worg mechanic runs for that character's journey, five worgs or twenty-five as 4a decides. What such a character meets informs Strahd only as any actual discovery would (below). The band does not turn back east: its road is west.

The protected crossing ends for the whole party at the first of these: the last Rhenari leaving the camp; the party discovering the deception and turning on the band, or the band fleeing it (1e); the party parting from the band, by going on alone, turning back or leaving the road; or the first sunup in the valley, whatever the band has done. The protection's ending, at sunup or otherwise, tells Strahd nothing. Turning back is an attempt to leave, and the gates "will not open for the PCs again until Strahd is destroyed" (p.6), in whatever company. From the moment the protection ends:
- the encounter check runs every three turns, Table 4 by day and Table 5 by night (3d, 3e);
- the party is in the woods at C, and its journey through them begins: the howling d6 runs every turn, and the worgs come, five or twenty-five as 4a decides, if five rounds of howling pass before the party leaves the woods. With the band gone in the last watch, the camp keeps only the watch the party set for itself; whether the howling wakes the sleepers is the DM's to determine from the circumstances, and the attack that follows uses the ordinary surprise rules. In most games the worgs come before dawn, and the party learns of the betrayal in the dark;
- Strahd's creatures ordinarily distinguish his Rhenari servants from the adventurers and do not deliberately attack them (3f). That protects only the Rhenari, held or free, and not from harm that happens to fall their way. A party that notices the wolves passing a held Rhenari by has found a clue;
- Strahd's knowledge of the party comes only from his reports and from what his servants actually discover and carry to him in play. The worgs at C take the chance to attack rather than carry word, so their discovering the abandoned party does not inform him. The first night's dusk and midnight reports do not locate the party (3g);
- there is no harassment that night. A strike or personal attack needs him to know where the party is, and he does not attack in person above ground by day (3g);
- at the dawn report, if a Rhenari has reached Tser Pool with the news, Strahd learns the party's last known position, where the band left it, not where it is now if it has moved; after that, the place it was last known governs (3g). If none has, the dawn report has the usual 60% chance, and the reports run as usual after it (3g);
- the harassment comes on the following night, wherever the party is (3f).

The DM can still give the protected night its unease: sounds in the trees, the band's silences, a horse that will not settle. None of it shows the party where it is. The players discover the betrayal through events: a journey that seemed safe, an empty camp and perhaps a missing possession, then the howling. The DM narrates what happens, not why.

### 1e. The camp and the Rhenari departure

The Rhenari stop for the night in a roadside clearing just inside the gates, short of where the body lies, a spot they have used before. The evening at the camp begins quietly, and the mood has tightened slightly without anyone naming why. Watches are set. The Rhenari take the last watch of the night, offered as a courtesy.

They mean to leave during that watch. The watchman is theirs, and lets the rest pack and move out without waking the party. Horses are led rather than ridden until the road bends. If the plan works, by dawn the Rhenari are gone, the wagons are gone, and one of the party's possessions is gone with them. The item is the DM's choice, calibrated to fit what the party is carrying: a signet ring, a sealed letter the party was delivering elsewhere, a prisoner in transit, a weapon of sentimental value, a pack animal with its load. The requirement is that the party wants it back but can continue without it.

**The night.** The night at the camp is the party's first night in Barovia, and it is protected until the Rhenari leave (1d). The hours after they have gone are a night in the open in the woods at C, without harassment; what Strahd can learn of the party, and when, is set out in 1d.

**If the departure is caught.** The plan is not guaranteed. A party that keeps its own watch, declines the courtesy, or has a familiar, a light sleeper or a listening thief may catch the Rhenari leaving or stealing. Caught, they try first to slip away, then to bluff, then to bargain (their statistics, Section 6). Under the guide's reading they fight only if their lives or traditions are threatened: they are "quick to act" when they are, and "merciless when they feel they must be" (p.32). A prevented theft leaves the item with the party. Rhenari who are held stay held, and none escapes without a reason. The road, the body, the letter and the village remain where I6 puts them, reached in whatever order the party goes; none of them is guaranteed. News of any bloodshed reaches the Rhenari camp at Tser Pool only if someone carries it: a Rhenari who escapes, another of Strahd's servants, or his own reports (3g).

The theft does three things. It gives the party a grievance, which is motivation. It gives them a thread, which is direction. And it sets up the encounter at the Rhenari camp at Tser Pool (G, p.11), where the item reappears and the Rhenari treat the matter as closed. If no item was taken, the thread is the Rhenari themselves.

### 1f. The body, the letter, and the arrival

The party has one road. It leads west, following the Rhenari tracks, and they take it.

Before the first half hour of walking is out, three turns past the gates (p.7), they find the body of a villager by the road. He has been there for days. Claw marks on the clothing, paw prints of many large wolves in the clay, a crumpled envelope in one hand. The letter is the Burgomaster's plea the module gives on p.7 continuing to p.8. Kolyan Indirovich, Burgomaster of Barovia, sealed with a large B, a week old. Ireena is dying. A vampire has held the valley for more than 400 years. The Burgomaster asks the recipients to seal Barovia from outside and save the world from its fate. There is wealth in the community, for those who return after its people are gone.

In the module's as-written opening this letter is the second letter and reveals the forgery. In the camouflaged opening it is the first and only letter, and its function shifts accordingly. It is not a revelation of betrayal but a dispatch from a dead man telling the party where they are and what holds the valley. It gives them a destination, the village where Ireena is, and a threat. The Burgomaster's instruction to seal the land from outside reads in this version as information the party will file away, because they are not trying to leave and do not yet know that leaving could kill them.

Later in the same morning, or by the early afternoon at latest, the road descends and the woods thin. The village of Barovia becomes visible through the fog: cobblestones, shuttered windows, a church on a rise at the base of the cliffs, Castle Ravenloft on its pillar above. The party enters.

## 2. The first day inside the valley

In the camouflaged opening, if the Rhenari departed, they are gone by morning, and a possession is missing only if the theft succeeded. If the party prevented their departure, the Rhenari remain, free or held. Little in the clearing resolves the deception. The road, the body and the village supply the answers in turn: direction, context, and a first fixed destination.

### 2a. What the party understands by noon

The opening has done its work when, by the time the party reaches the village, they understand four things.

First, they have been brought somewhere under false pretences.

Second, the road behind them is no longer a simple road home.

Third, the valley is held by a vampire with a history measured in centuries.

Fourth, the village ahead is not a neutral settlement but the centre of the present crisis.

That is the practical work of the opening, whether the DM uses the module as written or the camouflaged version. If one of those four has not landed by the time the party reaches the village, the opening has not yet finished its work, and the missing piece lands best before Ismark speaks.

### 2b. The body and the letter

The dead villager works best played plainly. He is not a mystery beat, and there is no theatrical flourish to find in him. He is there to tell the party what kind of place this is and what happens to people who try to carry messages out of it. His letter (1f, pp.7 to 8) is all they need here; the full cosmology can wait.

A careful player will notice the letter's date (a week old) and the state of the body (several days dead) and draw the obvious conclusion: the villager who carried this letter tried to leave, and something in the woods stopped him. That conclusion is correct. It is the party's first direct evidence that the valley is hostile to departure.

**Markov Gaardovich (the guide's addition).** I6 names no one. The guide makes the dead man Markov Gaardovich, a longstanding servant of the Burgomaster's household, known to Ismark and Ireena. The Burgomaster gave him the letter while still alive, about a week ago. Nearly a week ago the Burgomaster died under the assault on his house, the letter unsent, and since his death the wolves have not attacked the house (p.9). Markov hesitated, afraid, with his master unburied and the house's great holy symbol stolen, then left with the letter. The worgs caught him in the woods. He was loyal, not fearless, and he still hoped the warning might reach someone.

A cleric with *speak with dead* can question him: a cleric up to 7th level reaches a body dead up to a week, for 1 round and 2 questions, and a cleric of 7th or 8th level one dead up to a month, for 3 rounds and 3 questions, given a shared language (PHB p.48). He answers only from what he knew. He did not read the sealed letter and knows its purpose, not its words. He can say who sent him, that his master died before he left, that he delayed because he was afraid, and why he finally went. He cannot say what became of the household after he left, or anything of the fog's nature, and nothing obliges the DM to volunteer more than the questions draw out. The guide follows I6's own "dead for nearly a week" for the Burgomaster (p.9), which fits the letter's date; the module's figures of ten days (pp.8 and 9) are not used.

### 2c. The road and the first sight of the village

The descent into the village plays best as compression. The woods thin, the road narrows into settlement, the mist stops being merely atmospheric and starts feeling inhabited. The church and the castle are the two sightlines that matter. One promises sanctuary. The other promises the source.

The approach works best when the party arrives tired, suspicious, and short on certainty. If the woods have already drawn blood, the approach is stronger for it. Arriving too fresh weakens the module's opening pressure. A DM who has steered the party carefully through the crossing and the morning walk does not need to smooth the final approach. The party enters the village under the weight of what they have already learned, with the castle visible above and the absence of any other visible road behind them.

The opening ends here. Before moving into Part III, Sections 3 and 4 set out the valley procedures the DM will use from this point onward.

## 3. How to run the valley once they are in it

The valley has a day register and a night register, and the transition is absolute. By day, Barovia is oppressive. By night, Barovia becomes actively lethal. That is the governing distinction, and the DM's handling of everything in the valley that is not a specific scene in the module follows from it.

I6 does not say when these procedures begin. With the printed opening that matters, because the party spends a night at the inn and then marches five hours before it reaches the gates. The guide's ruling is to start them at the gates: no intelligence reports (3g), nightly harassment or encounter checks run at the inn or on the Old Svalich Road. Starting them earlier would let Strahd's machinery reach past the adventure's own threshold and show his hand before the opening has done its work.

The table gathers the valley's standing rules from the gates onward, with the guide's rulings marked. It is a navigation aid: the subsections state each rule with its sources, and where the two differ, the subsection governs.

### 3a. The fog

The fog functions as atmosphere inside the valley and as doom at the boundary. Its horror is retrospective: the party has already breathed it, and the danger is attached to exit, not to ordinary movement within Barovia. On leaving, each character must save vs. poison or start to choke, and a choking character who does not re-enter Barovia within 24 hours dies. The choking stops as soon as the character breathes the fog again (p.6).

Its use at the table is for sightlines, muffling, and mood. Spent too early, with every outdoor scene treated as poison gas, the effect is exhausted before the party discovers that leaving can kill.

I6 gives two ways out of the fog's hold. Strahd gave the Rhenari a potion that cancels its effects, not the fake potion they sell against him (p.32); the guide reads it as a lasting protection, which is why they alone pass through Barovia at will (pp.6 and 32). Madam Eva has buried it in a secret place, and the module says the party cannot discover it. The fog is Strahd's magic and disappears entirely upon his destruction (p.6); under the guide's reading, the poison already breathed ends with it. The rules supply a partial third, summarised for preparation in Part I 3b and given in full here. *Neutralize poison* cures only the poison present when it is cast (PHB p.48), and the fog infuses whoever breathes it (p.6), so inside the valley the cure lasts only until the next breath. A character cured outside the valley stays cured unless exposed again, by returning to the fog or otherwise; the spell gives no protection against a new exposure, and each character needs their own casting. Surviving the border is not by itself escaping the valley: curing the poison neither opens the gates nor makes a way through the woods (3b).

**What a searched or captured Rhenari yields is at 3d**, and the short answer is that the vial they carry and sell is a fake (p.32).

**How the fog works, for the DM (the guide's interpretation).** Breathing the fog deposits a poison that stays dormant while the character goes on breathing the valley's air. That air counts as the fog's air indoors and out, whether or not any mist is visible. Breathing air without the fog's influence is a withdrawal: leaving the valley is the ordinary case, and magically cleared air is another. A poisoned character who withdraws saves vs. poison; on a failure the choking begins, and it stops when the character breathes the fog again, though the poison remains. The save belongs to the withdrawal, not to the passing of time: a character who stays out of the fog's air makes no further save, and one who returns and withdraws again makes a fresh one. A character carrying no poison has nothing to activate. A choking character acts, moves and casts spells normally; I6 imposes no penalty short of its 24-hour consequence.

**Magic and the fog.** *Neutralize poison* is as above. The guide's rulings are marked; pages are cited for the printed rules they rest on.

**What the characters learn.** They experience symptoms, not the mechanism. A character who climbs into a *rope trick* and starts to choke, then recovers on climbing down, will naturally suspect the space rather than the air left behind, and may conclude that the spell, or such spaces in Barovia, are wrong. The characters experience only the choking and the recovery; the dormant poison is the DM's knowledge, not theirs. Further experiment, investigation or magic can reveal the truth, and the DM never withholds what a successful spell or ability would show.

### 3b. The gates

The gates function as threshold, not wall. The seal is enforced by fog, worgs, and the forest itself, not by invisible masonry. The gates open when the party comes within 50 feet and close behind it (p.7), and they "will not open for the PCs again until Strahd is destroyed" (p.6).

Map 1 shows no adjacent walls, and the boxed text puts the gates between stone buttresses that jut from woods it calls impenetrable (p.7). Beside the gate, the characters see what the boxed text and the map give them: buttresses, woods described as impenetrable, and no wall. Under the guide's reading, the word describes how formidable the woods are, not a further prohibition on movement: I6 names no cliff, drop or other continuous obstruction, and gives no rule for crossing them. Characters may leave the road and try to make their way through the trees, and the DM adjudicates the difficulty from the circumstances, which can make progress slow, dangerous or exhausting. Leaving the road is not leaving the woods: the worg mechanic and the encounter checks continue (4a, 3d). A party that inspects the gate and turns back has not tried to leave. Anyone who crosses back over the border, by whatever route, is choked by the fog (p.7) on the terms set out in 3a. The DM describes what is visible, neither inventing a wall nor promising that an attempt will succeed; an invisible wall costs the player's trust at the moment that trust is most load-bearing.

**A longer journey off the road.** For travel through the woods away from the road, the DMG's Becoming Lost rule applies (DMG p.49). A party that is not following a road or similar course, not guided by a creature who knows the country, and not using a well-drawn and correct map has a 7 in 10 chance of becoming lost in forest, checked at the start of each day's movement. A lost party moves in a direction other than the one intended: a d6 is read clockwise with the intended direction as 12 o'clock, 1 ahead to the right, 2 behind to the right, 3 or 4 directly behind, 5 behind to the left, 6 ahead to the left, and never the intended direction. A party that strays onto ground it has already travelled and mapped recognises the error at once. Otherwise the DM describes the terrain as though the party were moving as intended and checks again on the next day's movement. On the first day it is no longer lost, the party recognises that it has gone astray, but not where the mistake occurred; it can try to retrace its route to the last correctly mapped location or establish a new course. Under the guide's reading, a short detour around the buttresses does not trigger this daily travel check. Becoming lost affects navigation, not whether the woods can be traversed, and a lost party that strays across the border meets the fog like anyone else (3a).

**Magic and force at the gates (the guide's rulings).** The gates are "controlled by the will of Count Strahd" (p.6), not locked, barred or held. *Knock* opens locks, bars and held doors (PHB p.70), so it does not open them; *dispel magic* finds no magic named to negate, so it does not either; this is not a claim that the gates are non-magical. Characters can still go over or through. Flight, *levitate* and climbing pass over the buttresses and the gates, and nothing invisible stops them. Going round on foot means leaving the road for woods I6 calls impenetrable, adjudicated as above. The gates are "Huge iron gates" with "rusted bars" (p.7), and a character may try to bend the bars under the Strength table's Bend Bars/Lift Gates chance (PHB p.9), one attempt per character; success makes a gap without the gates opening. However the party passes, the fog lies beyond (3a), and getting past the gates is not escaping the valley.

### 3c. The village

The village is a community in active late-stage attrition, not a neutral quest hub.

The module's figures on p.8 are specific. Sixty percent of houses are occupied, usually by a man alone, sometimes his wife, rarely children. Shuttered windows. Claw marks on walls. Looted shops with nothing left in them. Only Bildrath's Mercantile and the Blood of the Vine tavern still operate. The church is kept safe at night by the priest's prayers (pp.1 and 9). The Burgomaster is dead. Ireena is the vampire's target.

This is the state of a community several steps into its last chapter. It does not need to be overexplained. The state of the village explains itself on sight, and a party walking through it for the first time reads it correctly without prompting.

The villagers themselves reinforce the reading. They never attack first, flee if possible, and at night hold lawful good holy symbols before them (p.8). The module does not say whether the symbols protect them. What the gesture shows is a population that has learned, over generations, what comes at night.

### 3d. The encounter check and the day register

**The encounter check.** Every three turns [30 minutes] the DM rolls a d6 wherever the party is in Barovia outside the castle, including when it shelters in a building (p.6). In the camouflaged opening no check is made while the party travels and camps with the Rhenari (1d). By day a 1 calls for Table 4; by night a 1 or 2 calls for a d12 on Table 5. A rolled encounter does not mean the creature automatically enters, or even notices, the occupants of a shelter. Under the guide's ruling, the DM determines what happens from the circumstances: whether it passes nearby, becomes aware of the party, or can reach them. Strahd's nightly harassment is separate and runs as I6 directs (3f).

By day, the valley is traversable. Worgs, villagers, Rhenari on the road, empty spaces, bad weather, distant signs of life. Day is when the party gathers bearings and makes decisions.

The day encounter table (Table 4, p.6) works less as a random generator than as a statement of register. Its result is rolled on a d8 (p.6), but the table lists only results 1 to 6, and I6 does not say what a 7 or 8 gives. The guide's reading is that a 7 or 8 is rolled again, which gives each printed entry an equal chance. The table produces worgs, Rhenari, and villagers in varying numbers, nothing undead, nothing overtly supernatural. Nothing on it makes the players think they are in a dungeon crawl yet. The horror is present, but not fully open.

**Table 4's three results, with what the DM needs to run them.**

**What a searched or captured Rhenari yields, which the party will ask.** **The potion they carry and offer to sell is a fake.** I6 is explicit: they readily tell adventurers they have a potion that protects them from the vampire, "although this is a lie", and they try to sell it for as much money as they can get (p.32). The **real** protection, Strahd's gift that cancels the fog, is **buried by Madam Eva in a secret place and cannot be discovered by the party** (p.6). A body on the road therefore yields money, goods and a worthless vial, and a party that drinks it and walks into the fog learns the difference the hard way. Anything the clan says is **misleading at best and often a lie** (p.32).

The table feeds atmosphere more than combat. An encounter with two villagers who flee on sight tells the party what kind of place this is. An encounter with four worgs in broad daylight on the road tells them what hunts here. The table is the valley introducing itself.

### 3e. The night register

By night, the valley is a different category of risk altogether.

The night table (Table 5, p.6) escalates hard, as it is meant to: worgs in larger numbers, zombies, bats in swarms, a ghost, ghouls, wights, wraiths. A 12 on the roll produces what the table calls a "maiden vampire": she drains two levels, charms with her gaze, and can turn gaseous as Strahd does. She is not Strahd. **This guide identifies her as Helga, the vampire of K32**, and 3i gives the identification, the evidence for it and everything that follows from it. The table's undead are turned under the procedure and rulings at 3h, with the **numbers at 3i**.

**What a result needs, and where it is.** The eight results and the rules that decide them are set out as reference at **3i**, with turning worked for the series' levels. At the table the DM needs three things from this section: that the table is live **from sundown to dawn**, that it escalates, and that **a victim killed by a ghoul, a wight or a wraith does not simply die** (U2, carried into Part III).

The lesson is strongest the first time the party encounters it. A night encounter rolled honestly on session one does more work than three lectures about how dangerous Barovia is at night.

### 3f. Strahd's nightly harassment

Strahd's harassment runs every night the party stays anywhere other than Castle Ravenloft (p.6); in the camouflaged opening it begins on the second night (1d). He arrives with 4d4 worgs and 10d10 bats, attacks for five rounds [5 minutes], and withdraws, leaving his hollow laughter in the distance. The worgs and bats are a finite, persistent population, and no attack deploys more than survive (4a; the guide's ruling).

His attacks are "intended more to frighten the PCs than to damage them" (p.6): he is fraying them, and the party learns, before the castle, that he attacks indirectly by preference (I).

Worgs test thresholds. If the party is indoors, the wolves try to break through a window or a door "every turn Strahd attacks": on a d6 roll of 1 or 2 a wolf breaks through, and once a wolf gets in, the rest of the wolves and bats come in (p.6). A turn is ten melee rounds (PHB p.39) and the attack lasts five, so under the guide's interpretation the wolves make one attempt at each nightly attack. The roll applies to any door or window of the party's shelter. *Hold portal* and *wizard lock* keep their own effects, but neither makes a door unbreakable (PHB pp.66 and 72), so the roll stands against a magically secured door as against a barred one; on the guide's inference, a secured door may send the wolves to a window instead. A character trying to force a door uses the PHB's Strength procedure, not the wolves' roll. I6 does not say why defences that have held before can give way now. The guide's reading is that this attack is Strahd's own, aimed at the party: a breach shows what happens in this attack, not that the house was never safe.

The bats get in the way of spellcasting: a character casting a spell with material components must make a dexterity check, rolling Dexterity or less on a d20 (p.6; the check, p.2). The check applies to a caster the bats are actually among, not to every caster in the encounter merely because bats are present; outdoors that can be at once, and indoors it follows a breach, when the bats come in with the wolves (the guide's reading of p.6). I6 does not say what a failed check does. Under the guide's ruling, a failed check interrupts the casting, and an interrupted spell is spoiled and lost, just as if it had been cast (DMG p.65).

**Strahd's creatures and his Rhenari servants.** Under the guide's ruling, Strahd's creatures ordinarily distinguish his Rhenari servants from the adventurers and **do not deliberately attack them**. That protects only the Rhenari, and not from harm that happens to fall their way. **The rule applies in both openings**: it matters in the camouflaged crossing (1d), and equally in the module's own opening where a held, charmed or accompanying messenger travels with the party into the valley and meets the worgs at C or the nightly harassment. A party that notices the wolves passing its companion by has found a clue.

Under the guide's reading, *protection from evil* does not keep the pack off. The harassment's worgs and bats are Strahd's servants, not creatures he summons (I6 has him attack "with his wolves and bats", p.6), so the spell's barrier against summoned creatures does not apply to them; its penalty against evil attackers applies to the worgs, which are evil. Creatures he does summon are kept out where the PHB's conditions are met (PHB p.44). A paladin's continuing protection from evil (PHB p.22) follows the same distinction; for its effect on his charm, see 3h.

Charm seeks invitation. The invitation rule binds Strahd: he cannot enter a building until someone invites him in (p.6), and he works on charming a character into doing so (3h). I6 gives no way to withdraw an invitation once it is given. Since I6 bars him only "until someone invites him in" (p.6), the guide reads the invitation as lasting: once he has been invited into a building, it remains open to him. This is the guide's ruling on I6's wording, not module text and not an AD&D rule.

The omission of Ireena is equally important. Neither Strahd nor his worgs ever attack Ireena Kolyana (p.6). A player who notices that the worgs harry everyone except her has seen one of the adventure's key truths before anyone explains it. The bats are not named in the module's line: they do no damage to anyone, and they reach her as they reach everyone else. Ismark knows that Strahd is drawn to his sister more than to anything else (p.8; Part III), but the evidence is on the floor before he says so. In the camouflaged opening, the Rhenari are passed over too (1d).

### 3g. Strahd's reports and strikes

Part I 4c explains how these work and why; this is their operative form.

From the party's arrival in Barovia (R; p.3 gives no starting point), Strahd's spies and servants report to him at dawn, noon, dusk and midnight; in the camouflaged opening the first night's reports do not locate the party (1d). Each time there is a 60% chance that he knows where the party is, and if he does, he attacks wherever it is within two hours, choosing the time and method of his attacks carefully (p.3). The DM tracks the report clock privately.

**Reports and strikes (the guide's ruling).** A successful report gives Strahd an opportunity to intervene, not an obligation. Although the report paragraph says he attacks within two hours, I6's general instructions say that whenever he is aware of the PCs' positions he "is allowed to make an attack how and where he wants" (p.3). The guide therefore allows him to withhold an attack, and he need not replace an abandoned attack with another. His three listed strikes are separate from the reports, available from the party's arrival (R) and each once in the adventure: he attacks a single PC for five melee rounds, then leaves; he calls 10 to 15 Strahd zombies, described on p.32, but deploys no more than survive in his reserve and any garrisons he withdraws (the guide's ruling); or he calls 3 to 12 worg wolves, drawn from the surviving worgs (4a). The zombies, or the worgs, all attack at the same time in the same place (p.3). Other encounters may involve his gaze, spells, summoning (3h), servants or personal combat, including fighting when the party attacks him. The first strike differs because it commits him to attacking one PC for five melee rounds and then leaving. Outside that strike, the guide imposes no fixed combat duration: Strahd chooses whether to press an attack or withdraw, according to his goals and the circumstances. The strikes are also separate from the nightly harassment (p.6; 3f), and spent strikes go on the running record (3h).

**By day.** Vampires "must rest in a coffin or similar receptacle during hours of sunlight unless far beneath the surface of the ground, and in the latter case they must occasionally return to such rest" (MM p.99), and Barovia's clouds give no exemption (I): above ground he does not act by day. His tomb, K86, lies below the catacombs, which are "buried deep beneath the keep" (p.27): Map 12 shows a stair down to it, and the text gives the teleports between crypt 32 and K86 (pp.27, 29 and 30). The Tome has him "far below Ravenloft" (p.31): there he is far beneath the surface and can conduct his affairs by day (I). The spies "report to him four times each day (at dawn, noon, dusk, and midnight)" (p.3), and the reports reach him underground at those times; while he rests, his servants hold routine reports until he is available, may wake him for urgent ones, and raise the alarm at an immediate threat (R). The two-hour window runs from the scheduled report time, even when the report is held; once that window expires, the opportunity lapses (R). His servants act above ground by day on his orders (R); a personal attack above ground cannot come by day (MM p.99). Direct sunlight remains lethal to him.

**Concealment.** A report does not reveal characters concealed where Strahd's servants cannot find them, as in a *rope trick*. Characters who enter such concealment after a successful report may still be sought at their last known location, but cannot be found there while they remain concealed.

Between interventions he is in his Fortunes place, where he "can always be found" (p.3), unless he has been forced to his tomb. Under the guide's ruling that place is his default and his return point, not a second place he occupies while he is elsewhere; the 90% lair chance is not rolled. It sets where he is encountered during the hours he can act; by day he is somewhere far beneath the surface, such as his tomb, and an above-ground place finds him absent (I). Of the reading's places, the Crypt of Sergei (K85) lies with the deep catacombs, and he can be found there by day (I). The Crypt of Ravenovia (K88) has "Great stained windows filling the eastern walls" that "filter dim light into this room" (p.30): it admits daylight, so by day he is absent from it (I), though filtered daylight is not thereby the direct sunlight that destroys him (MM p.99). The K88 reading qualifies p.3's "can always be found" for the hours of daylight.

**Turning.** No cleric below 6th level can turn a vampire. A 6th-level cleric needs 20 on a d20, a 7th-level cleric 16; paladins turn as a cleric two levels lower (DMG p.75; PHB p.22). Each cleric present may make an attempt (DMG p.65). A successful turn drives him directly away for 3 to 12 rounds; he can come back, and is then subject to further turning (DMG p.76). A failed attempt bars that cleric from trying again against him (DMG pp.65 and 76). Under the guide's ruling, the bar lasts for the encounter, and the cleric may try again in a genuinely later one; a brief withdrawal followed at once by the same confrontation is not a new encounter. The DMG states no duration. A bar for the rest of the adventure would leave a party's one turning cleric a single attempt against Strahd across the whole module, and would make I6's Icon of Ravenloft, +4 to turn undead (p.14), useless against him to a cleric who had already failed. When Strahd is present with lesser undead that follow or serve him, the DM may disallow any turning of the group if the cleric cannot affect him; the rule never applies to mindless undead such as skeletons and zombies (DMG p.65). Against lesser undead, a successful turn affects 1 to 12 of them (DMG p.76). Strahd zombies are turned as mummies (p.32): a 5th-level cleric needs 16, a 6th-level 13, a 7th-level 10 (DMG p.75). I6 lists their intelligence as "Non-" (p.32), so they are mindless undead for the mixed-group rule: a cleric who cannot turn Strahd may still try to turn his zombies.

**His gaze.** He charms a character who meets his gaze; the victim saves against spells at -2 (p.3; MM p.99). Eye contact is needed (p.3; MM p.99).

**Avoiding his gaze (the guide's ruling).** *Monster Manual II* (p.55) gives a gaze-contact procedure for a dracolisk or any other creature using a gaze weapon, with 1 added for a man-sized creature. Applied to man-sized Strahd, a character viewing him has a 6 in 10 chance of meeting his gaze, one attacking normally 4 in 10, and one deliberately avoiding his gaze 2 in 10; surprise raises the chance to 8 in 10, or 10 in 10 if completely surprised. A character avoiding his gaze in melee attacks at -4, as MM2 gives for the dracolisk. Under the guide's ruling he may direct his charming gaze at one character each round, in addition to his blow: I6 has him charm "at will" (p.3), and MM p.99 lists the gaze apart from his attack. The target makes the contact roll, and the saving throw if the gaze is met. He cannot turn a separate charming gaze on every character in the same round, so charming a party takes time and is never certain. The contact roll decides whether the gaze is met; the saving throw, at -2 (p.3), or at a net 0 under *protection from evil* (below), decides whether the charm takes hold.

Under *Unearthed Arcana*, a cavalier, and a paladin as a cavalier sub-class, has a 90% chance that the charm has no effect, in addition to its saving throw (UA pp.15 and 16); under the guide's ruling the 90% is rolled first, and the save only if that fails (R); a paladin under the Players Handbook alone does not. A charmed victim obeys his commands, including handing over possessions and betraying companions, unless an instruction is obviously self-destructive (DMG p.65). Commands reach the victim within sight, or within 60 feet out of sight; without a shared language, only basic emotions and simple commands pass. A magic circle of protection breaks his link with the victim while the victim stays inside it, so that the charm seems broken; it is suppressed, not ended, and his control returns once the victim is outside, unless the charm has been magically dispelled or has worn off (DMG p.65). Under the guide's ruling, *protection from evil*, which encircles its recipient and is cast by tracing a circle (PHB p.44), counts as such a circle while it lasts. The spell also gives +2 on saving throws caused by the attacks of evil creatures (PHB p.44); under the guide's ruling that includes the save against his gaze, so a protected character saves at a net 0.

**The paladin's protection (the guide's reading).** A paladin's continuing protection from evil extends 10 feet (PHB p.22). For Strahd's charm it counts as the circle above: it suspends his control over a charmed character within it, without ending the charm, and gives the spell's +2 on the save against his gaze. The paladin's own +2 on all saving throws (PHB p.22) is a separate class benefit and applies in addition, to the paladin alone. Against his gaze, an unprotected character saves at -2, one within the aura at a net 0, and the paladin at a net +2. A protected character still makes the contact roll and the save; if the save fails, the charm takes hold, but he cannot command the character while they remain within the aura, and his control returns, without another gaze, if they leave it. The same holds for a charm already in place when a character enters or leaves the aura. The aura does not stop him attacking physically or turning his gaze on anyone. A party that keeps within 10 feet of its paladin has a real defence against his charm, at the cost of clustering in a small area, the moment his *fireball* is held for (Part I 4b).

**How long the charm lasts.** MM p.99 has a vampire's victims behave as if struck by *charm person*, and that spell's duration is tied to a saving throw checked at intervals set by the victim's Intelligence; a success breaks it (PHB pp.55 and 65). DMG p.65 makes a monster's charm more powerful than the spell in the control it gives, and says only that it will eventually wear off. Applying the spell's schedule to Strahd's charm is the guide's reading; the intervals themselves are the PHB's. The -2 belongs to resisting his gaze, so the periodic saves are made without it, and neither his Hit Dice nor his level as a magic-user alters the intervals (the guide's ruling):

| **6-7** | **10 to 100 (10d10) bats.** AC 6; MV 4"/18"; HD 1d4; **#AT 0; Dmg 0**; AL N (p.6). **Table 5 gives them no attacks and no damage.** The guide treats their movement as **briefly confusing and obscuring the party's view**, drawing on the Monster Manual's description of bats summoned by a vampire (MM p.99). **This is an atmospheric interpretation, not an additional attack or mechanical penalty.** I6's dexterity check for casting belongs to **Strahd's nightly harassment**, where the bats come in with the wolves through a breach, and is at **3f**; it is not carried here |
| **8** | **1 ghost.** AC 0 materialised, 8 ethereal; MV 9"; HD 10; Dmg ageing 10 to 40 years; AL LE (p.6). **The sight of it ages a humanoid 10 years and sends them fleeing in panic for 2 to 12 turns unless a save versus magic succeeds** (MM p.43); clerics above 6th level are immune. **No cleric below 7th level can affect it at all**; a 7th needs 20 (DMG p.75) |
| **9** | **3 to 12 (3d4) ghouls.** AC 6; MV 9"; HD 2; Dmg 1-3/1-3/1-6; AL CE (p.6). **Paralysis on any hit unless the save succeeds; elves are immune to it.** Subject to all attack forms **except *sleep* and *charm***. A magic circle of *protection from evil* keeps them wholly at bay (MM pp.43, 44). Turned: 5th and 6th automatically, 7th destroys (DMG p.75) |

| **11** | **1 to 4 wraiths.** AC 4; MV 12"/24"; HD 5+3; Dmg 1-6; AL LE; **a hit drains one energy level** (p.6). **Silver does half damage; only magical weapons do full. No power in full sunlight** (MM p.102). Turned: 5th needs 13, 6th needs 10, 7th needs 7 (DMG p.75) |
| **12** | **1 maiden vampire.** AC 1; MV 12"/18"; HD 8+3; Dmg 5-10 **and two levels drained**; AL CE; gaze can charm; becomes gaseous as Strahd does (p.6). +1 or better weapons to hit, regenerates 3 a round (MM p.99). **No cleric below 6th level can affect her**; 6th needs 20, 7th needs 16 (DMG p.75) |

**A victim killed by a ghoul, a wight or a wraith does not simply die.** What happens is at U2 in the master reference and is carried into Part III.

**Two guide rulings stand behind this result, and both are the guide's.** Everything below follows from them and not from I6.

**Ruling one: the maiden vampire is Helga.** I6's Table 5 names no individual and gives her no hit points and no lair. This guide identifies her as **Helga, the vampire of K32** (p.18). **What supports it:** the statistics match exactly; Helga is **not confined to K32**, being a townsperson's daughter who chose a life of evil with Strahd and who will leave with a party that asks her to; and a wandering night result is exactly how a party meets her **away from the castle**. **What it does not amount to:** I6 names only **two individual** vampires, Strahd and Helga, but it is not true that the module has no others, since **Table 7 gives 1 to 4 unnamed vampires in the castle, "old, hapless victims of the Count"**. Those are castle encounters rather than valley wanderers, and the matching statistics are **the standard Monster Manual line**, which both would share. **So the match makes the identification possible rather than certain. It is adopted for continuity, and it is a ruling.**

**Ruling two: her coffin is in K32.** **I6 gives her no coffin at all.** This guide places a concealed one in K32, because the vampire rules require a vampire to have one; U3.4 carries it in the master reference, and **U3.4 is the guide's ruling written down, not printed module information**.

**What follows, and only from those two rulings.** Her **36 hit points** come from her keyed entry at p.18, not from Table 5. The destruction outcome below depends on **both** rulings: if she is not Helga, or if a DM gives her a coffin elsewhere, the arithmetic has a different answer. **If she is destroyed in a wandering encounter she cannot afterwards appear at K32**, and the DM strikes her from the castle.

**If a vampire is brought to 0 hit points in Part II.** The procedure is the same for Helga and for Strahd, and the outcome is the same, but the two are adjudicated separately.
1. **Nothing is destroyed at 0.** The vampire **is not killed**; it is **forced into gaseous form** (S6.1, MM p.99). What the party sees is the body collapsing into gas and the gas **leaving**, at 3" a round, 30 yards in the open (S6.4). That is all they see.
2. **The twelve turns then run** (S6.1). **The gas is bound to the ground and cannot climb to the castle** (S6.4): **its way back is by F and the southern road, which is too long to cover in 12 turns** (S6.6). **Strahd's coffin is at K86, which I6 prints** (p.30); **Helga's is at K32 by the guide's ruling above**. Both are in the castle, and **neither is reachable from Part II's road, the woods at C or the camp**.
3. **So the vampire is destroyed**, at the end of the twelve turns, out of the party's sight.
4. **The party does not automatically know it.** They witnessed a retreat, not a death. **Nothing in Part II tells them the outcome**, and a DM should not announce it. A party that assumes it killed something has assumed it.
5. **Two things can change the outcome:** a **strong wind already established** that carries the gas, and a **coffin the DM has placed elsewhere**. Neither arises by default.

**On S6.6's carriage.** Part II carries **the road provision only**. The village provision, and the whole of the gaseous-movement procedure, are carried **in full in Part III**, whose wording this agrees with. The duplication is deliberate: this is partial carriage under the series rule.

## 4. The Svalich woods

On later traversal, the woods leave marks on the party. They are the pressure corridor between shelter and exposure, and the module's worg mechanic comes into its own once the village is behind the party. The module prints it for the woods at C, between the gates and the village (p.8), so it applies directly to the road east in retreat. Under the guide's ruling it runs there only: this clarifies the module's geographical scope rather than adding a mechanic. Elsewhere in the Svalich woods, on the north fork to Tser Pool and the road west, the ordinary encounter checks supply the wolves (3d); extending the special procedure there would leave its two pack sizes without an answer.

### 4a. The worgs

The wolf howls function as tempo rather than as a cue to roll for combat. The logic sits on p.8. In the woods at C, a d6 is rolled every turn [every 10 minutes]: on a 4 or higher, one wolf cries in the distance. Another cry joins each round [each minute]. If the party is still in the woods after five rounds of howling, the worgs attack: five if the PCs are entering Barovia, twenty-five if they are attempting to leave (p.8). The attack stops if the party leaves the woods by entering the village. The encounter check (3d) runs alongside. In the camouflaged opening the mechanic does not run while the party travels and camps with the Rhenari (1d). The party hears wolves. What reaches them is worgs: AC 6; MV 18"; HD 4+4; #AT 1; Dmg 2-8; AL NE (p.8).

**Which pack (the guide's ruling).** Twenty-five worgs attack when the party is making an attempt to leave Barovia, including trying to pass the gates or to find another route across the border. Five attack when it is travelling towards the village, or returning towards the gates without attempting to leave. The DM determines which applies from what the characters are doing, not from the direction they face; the players need not announce an escape for their actions to establish one.

I6 does not say whether the howling begins again after an attack. Read literally, "every turn the PCs are in the woods" never stops, and a long road through them would bring attack after attack. The guide's reading is one attack for each journey through the woods: once the worgs have attacked, the mechanic does not run again until the party leaves the woods and enters them again. It plays better: the five-round warning keeps its meaning, the woods stay a single test of the party's arrival rather than a grind, and I6's contrast between five worgs entering and twenty-five leaving survives. A DM who prefers the literal reading keeps rolling, and the finite population (below) is then the only limit. A new journey means genuinely leaving the woods and later re-entering them; stepping briefly out of them during an encounter already under way, other than by entering the village, does not begin a new journey or a new attack. Crossing back past the gates is an attempt to leave Barovia, with the fog's terms (3a).

The sound gathers, and the five rounds are real preparation time. The party can choose its ground, organise a withdrawal, ready weapons and protective or battlefield magic, or simply run for the village. The ground limits all of it. In area C "The tree trunks almost touch. The thick, damp undergrowth presses in on you, making it impossible even to see one another at all times" (p.7), and forest shortens encounter distance (DMG p.49). Sightlines are short and missile fire has little room. The DM uses the forest terrain and encounter-distance rules to establish where the approaching pack can first be seen. When the larger pack is triggered, all twenty-five worgs attack, as I6 says, if twenty-five survive; how many can engage the characters at once follows from that ground. The point is that the road is already being hunted, and the party has time to hear it coming.

**Strahd's worgs and bats (R).** The worgs are a persistent population, established once before play: the 25 that guard the woods at C, in packs of 9, 8 and 8, and 4d4 more, rolled once, in packs of no more than 12. That reads I6's two printed worg quantities together, the force at C (p.8) and the nightly harassment (p.6); I6 gives no total. A random worg result meets one surviving pack, at no more than its survivors, and never several packs assembled to match the roll. The nightly harassment, the worg strike and the force at C roll their printed numbers and draw on the survivors of all the packs, which Strahd can redeploy; no encounter deploys more worgs than remain. The 3,000 bats of K84 (p.27) supply the nightly bats, the 10d10 drawn from the colony's survivors. Killed animals are removed permanently, from whichever group they came, and survivors keep their wounds until healed. The ordinary wolves he summons (MM p.99) are not worgs: summoning calls existing wolves, not replacements for the dead, and I6 gives no count of them. A party that destroys the worgs before it tries to leave meets fewer at C, or none.

**Worgs and the animal spells (the guide's rulings).** Worgs are evil, language-using "neo-dire wolves" (MM p.101). *Speak with animals* reaches only "basically normal, non-fantastic creatures" (PHB p.46), and worgs do not qualify; ordinary wolves do, including those Strahd summons, and then the addressed wolf and others of its kind with it do not attack while the spell lasts. *Invisibility to animals* has its own test, "normal animals with intelligence under 6", excluding those with magical abilities (PHB p.55): worgs have no magical abilities, and their "Semi-low" intelligence is read as under 6, so they do not perceive the recipient. It protects that character, not the party. *Animal friendship* does not work on them. *Tongues* and *speak with monsters* let the party talk with worgs, whose response follows the spell's own terms and their service to Strahd.

### 4b. The asymmetry

The two pack sizes (p.8) are the module stating its directional truth in mechanics. A party that tests retreat learns the valley's direction on the road it came down, with the worgs between it and the gates, and closed gates and the fog beyond (3a, 3b).

### 4c. What the woods are for

The damage is meant to land. Five HD 4+4 worgs do not overwhelm a balanced party at levels 5 to 7, but they cost hit points and spell slots, and they tell the party something a fresh arrival at the village does not know. Twenty-five are a substantially different threat to a party of that level, which is the asymmetry's point (4b). The woods are not an obstacle to get past. They are the adventure introducing its terms.

## 5. Where Part II stops

The clean stopping point is the first settled understanding of Barovia's operating rhythm:

the road in, the body, the letter, the village, day as oppressive, night as lethal, retreat as false option.

That is a complete Part II. The party is inside the valley, has a destination, has a threat named, has the valley's two registers distinguished, and has seen or at minimum can infer the cost of trying to leave. Everything else belongs to the village, to the road up, to the castle, to the climax.

Part III takes the party into the village proper from here, to Ismark, Ireena, the church, Bildrath, and the first social choices.

## 6. Before session one

Part I Section 3e lists what is settled before play, the Fortunes reading and Strahd's opening plan among it. Part II adds only what its own opening needs:

1. **The opening.** The module's or the camouflage.
2. **If the camouflage:** the hamlet, Anshel Rhen's offer, the item the Rhenari will take, whether any of the band differs from the clan's neutral evil, and the band's size (1b, 1c, 1e). I6's Rhenari statistics differ between entries (Table 4, p.6, gives HD 6; the camp at G, p.11, ten 4th-level fighters). For the band, the guide takes the clan's own entry: AC 6; MV 12"; HD 5; #AT 1; Dmg 1-8; intelligence high; AL NE (p.32).
3. **The running record** ready for the first night (3h). It is **the handover to Part III**, which inherits it field for field, so it is kept during play and not reconstructed at the end.
