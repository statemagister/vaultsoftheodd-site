*Dwellers of the Forbidden City* is remembered as a Conan story converted into a dungeon, and its author has said as much himself. That memory is accurate, and it hides most of what the module actually is.

This article tests that memory. Because the pulp ancestry is real and acknowledged, the way to find out what I1 contributes is not to list resemblances but to try to take the module apart: read the lost-city tradition it comes from, give back everything that tradition can claim, and see what is left standing at the end.

A great deal goes back. What does not is stranger and smaller than the module's reputation suggests.

## The control

Cook has been explicit about his sources. In a Dragonsfoot question and answer thread he said the adventure began in a campaign he ran before joining TSR, and that "it wasn't a city then", only the final ruins in a jungle. He wanted a pulpy Conan feel of fabulous treasure, decadent ruin and eldritch horror, and said he was pretty much ripping off Howard's "Red Nails". The main hall was his TSR writing sample. Asked about the same resemblance in a 2009 interview, this time prompted by his interviewer rather than unprompted, he confirmed it: Red Nails was clearly what he was going for, it is his favourite Conan story, and the city was based on it.

That interview also supplies his reading. As a boy he read Conan, Solomon Kane, most of the Tarzan novels, Doc Savage, the Shadow, Vance and Lovecraft, with Leiber's Fafhrd and Grey Mouser among his favourites. The Tarzan novels matter here for a reason that appears below.

The risk in describing I1's cultural register is obvious. A jungle lost city is a pulp genre with deep stock, and it would be easy to collect exotic-looking objects from the module and call the collection a cultural direction when the objects are simply what such stories contain. So three of those stories were read in full, and everything they can claim was given back to them.

**Red Nails**, Howard, 1936, the acknowledged source. Xuchotl lies beyond an impassable forest, its quarters held by hostile groups, a disputed centre between them, catacombs beneath, its occupants living in architecture they did not build. Its builders are Eastern. Conan reads the friezes and names Vendhya or Kosala; the city's history says outright that they came from the east, from Old Kosala. Vendhya is Howard's India, Kosala a real Indian kingdom, and the walls are jade identified against Khitan caravan loot, Khitai being Howard's China. The Nahuatl-derived names belong to the Tlazitlans, who arrived later from Stygia and killed the remnant they found.

**Jewels of Gwahlur**, Howard, 1935, unacknowledged and closer in places. Cliffs rise sheer from jungle enclosing a circular forested plain three or four miles across, ringed without a break. The valley is taboo, approached by no one but priests, and only they know the secret entrance. The builders are gone, absorbed by surrounding tribes, and conflicting legends explain their downfall. There is a monumental bronze door with a great carved head worked around it, a gong of gold with a mallet sounding in the empty palace, bamboo and thatched huts.

**Tarzan and the Forbidden City**, Burroughs, 1938, also unacknowledged, and the one Cook is on record as having had within reach. Ashair is called the Forbidden City throughout, sits in a hidden valley sharing a lake with a rival city, is run by priests, and admits outsiders who are then not permitted to leave. That Cook names the Tarzan novels among his boyhood reading does not show he took anything from this one, but it does remove the objection that he is unlikely to have met it.

## What the controls take back

A great deal, and it should be conceded plainly.

The cliff-ringed valley is Howard's. So is the secret entrance, the taboo jungle, the gong, bamboo and thatch, and the monumental bronze doorway with a monstrous head worked into it, which bears on I1's Hall of the Serpent God and its bronze-plated doors with a cobra-headed man above them.

So is the pattern of contradictory origins. I1 says three incompatible things about the mongrelmen: descendants of the slaves once kept in the city, all that remains of the original human inhabitants, and the last human descendants of the yuan-ti ancestors. Alone that reads as an author who never decided. But Red Nails has three population layers and Gwahlur has conflicting legends about the vanished race stated as such. The contradiction has a shape its ancestors already possess.

The title is not exclusively anyone's. A pulp lost city called the Forbidden City was in print in 1938, in a series Cook says he read most of.

And the Eastern element itself is in the acknowledged source. Howard's builders are Indic-coded, and Howard had assembled them from a mixed repertoire of his own.

## The hinge

None of that changes what I1 put on the page.

Provenance is not cultural direction. Where an element came from and what it contributes to the character of the society on the page are separate questions, and answering the first does not answer the second. A gong is still a gong in the published module whether Cook took it from Howard or not. Bamboo is still bamboo. Howard's Eastern builders do not become less Eastern for being Howard's, and the register they belong to does not stop operating when it crosses into Greyhawk.

What the controls establish is how careful to be about authorship. They stop us crediting Cook with invention wherever an antecedent exists, which is often. They leave untouched the question of what cultural material the published module contains, and they leave one further finding, which is that some of the specification survives all three.

## What I1 presents

The society in the Forbidden City is furnished, throughout, from an Asian-coded repertoire.

Some of it has antecedents. Bamboo builds the bullywug stockade, screens the paths in Horan's garden and arches over his lily pond, and is sharpened into pungi stakes at the tunnel mouths. A large brass gong and striker sit under a thatched roof on the bullywug platform. Canoes serve at the sacrificial pool and on the lake.

Some of it does not. Rice sits in a storeroom and rice paper on a writing platform. Rice-straw matting covers floors in three separate places. Doors are brightly painted sliding paper panels. A square stone fire pit is sunk in a wood floor beneath a smoke hole, with a kettle on an iron frame above it. There are low tables and silken cushions, a shelf of statuettes, tubs of pickled plums in the pantry. On a reception platform, a flat cushion, an inkpot of red ink, two brushes, an inking stone and a sheet of fine rice paper. In a bedroom, scroll paintings. And at A2, in a sacrificial cave, a statue of a snake-bodied, six-armed woman between charcoal braziers on tripods, which is placeable: a human upper body joined to serpent coils is one of the standard naga forms in Indic religious art, and multi-armed divinity is likewise Indic.

None of that second group appears in Red Nails, in Jewels of Gwahlur, or in Ashair. Burroughs comes nearest and misses instructively. The one lacquer object in his novel is a red lacquer Buddha in a curtained back room behind a curio dealer's shop in an African port, among teak furniture and incense. It belongs to the colonial bazaar in his opening chapters, not to the Forbidden City.

That second group is also coherent in kind. Rice is eaten, written on and slept above. The hearth and kettle say how the house is warmed. Low tables say how the room is used. Pickled plums say what is in the pantry. Inkstone, brushes and scrolls give a writing and picture culture. The statue gives a devotional image. These are not exotic nouns scattered across ruins. They describe how people live.

The pulp lost-city repertoire, in these three instances, furnishes ruins. It does not furnish households.

One qualification travels with that. No antecedent found means none found in three controlled texts. It does not mean Cook invented these details, and it does not mean they have no ancestor anywhere in his reading.

## Selection, administration, and date

**The pan lung** sits outside the pattern and is useful for it. Its immediate provenance is the Fiend Folio, so an antecedent plainly exists. What is Cook's is the choice of that creature, from a large bestiary, for a lake in this city. And the cultural signal is not one we have to supply, because the module's own text calls it a type of oriental dragon. It is clean evidence that a further Asian-coded element was selected into a register already running, and evidence of nothing else.

**Movement is administered.** Horan issues gold tablets which his agents show the Main Entrance guards to prove they are on his business, and the guards at C11 will attack anyone attempting to enter or leave without one. Set against the controls this is a different kind of thing. Alkmeenon is restricted by taboo and priestly monopoly of a secret way. Ashair is restricted by prohibition. Xuchotl is sealed by circumstance. I1 has an authority issuing portable credentials that a third party can carry, which guards recognise, governing passage in both directions.

The name deserves care here. Burroughs means the title cannot show that Cook took it from Beijing, and no route of transmission is recoverable. But the phrase carries its meaning from the historical Forbidden City, a restricted imperial seat, and that meaning was already available to Burroughs. I1 realises it. Its city is not merely a dangerous place outsiders avoid; it is a seat of authority to which admission is administered. The provenance is unrecoverable and the semantic correspondence is not.

**And the register is dated.** Two of the surviving details sit in areas A1 to A10, which were the Origins 1980 tournament, a timed run of three and a half hours with everything else ignored. A6 is named the Hall of Meditation and has rice-straw matting on part of its floor. A2 holds the six-armed statue. So the cultural specification is not a flourish added when a tournament dungeon was expanded into a book. It is present by the summer of 1980, and Horan's house develops it much more densely afterwards.

## Limits

None of this recovers intention. Cook has said he had a passion for oriental history, but he said it in 2009 about advising on a project in the mid-eighties, and it cannot be carried back to 1980 on evidence that does not exist. The distribution of Asian-coded choices across I1 is a fact about the module; why they were made is not in any of these documents.

Nor does it establish who made which change. Cook's home campaign, the tournament version and the published expansion are three stages, and Harold Johnson and Lawrence Schick hold development credits on the last.

And it does not make Cook the inventor of a lost city. Almost nobody in that tradition was.

## What was left behind

What I1 supplies, once the tradition has taken back everything it can claim, is a society whose furnishings, worship, writing and household arrangements are drawn from an Asian repertoire, with an explicitly oriental dragon in its lake and a bureaucracy at its gate.

That is what I1 contributed to Greyhawk, and it survives the finding that Cook invented very little of the machinery around it. The cliffs, the hidden entrance, the taboo, the bronze doors, the gong, the contradictory history and even the name were all in the tradition before him. What was not is the interior: matting on a floor, a kettle over a sunken hearth, plums in a tub, an inkstone and two brushes on a low platform, a six-armed goddess between braziers, and tablets checked at a gate.

What became of that direction afterwards is a question about publication history rather than about I1, and it needs the later sources read on their own terms before anything is said about it. What can be said now is that it was there, that it was there by the summer of 1980, and that it has been sitting in an out-of-print module ever since, largely unread as anything but a dungeon.
