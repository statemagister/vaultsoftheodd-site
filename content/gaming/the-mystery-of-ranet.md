*Ranet appears once in Dwellers of the Forbidden City, David Cook's 1981 module I1. The name may preserve an unexpected connection to Ursula K. Le Guin, but the adventure leaves a mystery that may no longer have an answer.*

*Evidence: I1, collated across both 1981 printings; the Monster Manual (1977); the Players Handbook (1978); Deities & Demigods (1980); the Fiend Folio (1981); Ursula K. Le Guin, The Tombs of Atuan (1971); J. Viaud, Egyptian Mythology (1965), from the Larousse Encyclopedia of Mythology (1959); the British Museum, A Guide to the Third and Fourth Egyptian Rooms (1904); E. A. Wallis Budge, The Book of the Dead: The Papyrus of Ani (1895). Later Greyhawk material is named only so that it can be set aside.*

## The name beneath the city

At the back of I1, under campaign play, Cook offers four adventures for referees to develop. The first goes beneath the Forbidden City into an ancient sewer, and the sewer is not empty: "In it now live monsters and colonies of creatures of all sorts." The most common of them are jungle-ghouls, more cunning than ordinary ghouls and led by small demonic leaders, and the last human descendants of the yuan-ti's ancestors. The two peoples wage a constant war through the tunnels, "attacks, sallies, counter-attacks, and sieges", the ghouls trying to turn the humans into twisted slime-beasts with a cancerous touch.

The humans are nearly blind after centuries underground and live by their other senses. They hate those who bring light. They worship a "kindly god of darkness who drives away the demons of light".

Then, in two sentences: somewhere in the tunnels, it is rumoured, lies "the fabulous lost temple of Ranet"; and, to the referee, "The temple is there, and is guarded by the giant snake-queen."

That is the only time the name appears in the module. It invites a question. Who, or what, is Ranet?

A reader will usually answer it before asking it. "Temple of Ranet" reads naturally as a temple dedicated to a god called Ranet; the god these people worship is the obvious candidate; and a serpent in the sanctuary looks like part of the cult. Every one of those connections is supplied by the reader.

There are two ways to test them. One asks where the name came from. The other asks what the module allows it to be. They do not yield the same amount of answer.

## A passage that recalls another book

Taken item by item, the passage is the furniture of fantasy: an underground temple, a guardian, darkness, treasure. Matching items proves nothing. But the way these items stand to one another recalls a particular book, Ursula K. Le Guin's The Tombs of Atuan, published in 1971.

Its protagonist, Tenar, is the One Priestess of the Nameless Ones: taken as a child, held to be the first priestess reborn, her whole identity belonging to the place. The powers she serves are not worshipped there from elsewhere; they are bound to the Tombs themselves. Access to the underground is graded, and its innermost part is hers alone. Light is forbidden below. When an outsider comes seeking the treasure hidden there, the powers make the place dangerous to him, and it is Tenar who finds him, traps him and decides what becomes of him.

Beside that, Cook's passage has a lost and fabulous sacred place underground; a single female figure guarding it, whom he calls a queen; a people nearby for whom darkness is holy; and an adventure whose whole point is that outsiders will come looking. The resemblance is in the arrangement, not the objects.

And it does not depend on the name. It is there in the description alone.

## Ranet backwards

The name, read backwards, is Tenar.

The reversal is exact: five letters, no respelling, nothing dropped or added, nothing moved except the order. On its own it might be coincidence; five-letter names are cheap, and some will read backwards into something. But it does not stand on its own. The description already recalls The Tombs of Atuan, and the one unexplained name in it points at the same book independently. Two separate observations converging on one source are harder to dismiss than either would be alone.

Reversal was also an established naming habit in the circle Cook was writing for. Drawmij, whose instant summons appeared in the Players Handbook in 1978, is Jim Ward spelled backwards. Yrag, Gary Gygax's own fighter, is Gary spelled backwards. Zagyg belongs to the same family of jokes, though it is reverse-derived from Gygax rather than an exact reversal.

None of this shows that Cook read Le Guin. In the interviews and question-and-answer material searched for this piece, no example of Cook reversing a name has been found, and no reference by him to Le Guin, Earthsea or Tenar. His account of his boyhood reading, given in 2009, names Howard, Burroughs, Vance, Lovecraft and others and does not include her; but he gave it as a recollection, not an inventory, so its silence is not evidence either way. What the convergence gives is a lead strong enough to test.

## Tenar taken apart

If Cook did build from Tenar, the interesting question is not what he borrowed but where it went.

In Atuan the relationships are explicit. One woman holds the name, the office, the bond to the sacred place and the guardianship of what lies within, and the powers dwell in the place she serves. In Cook, comparable elements appear, but separated. The name is attached to the temple: it is Ranet's. The guardianship, the figure who is there when intruders come, belongs to the snake-queen. The darkness belongs to the people outside and to their god, whom Cook does not name and does not place in the temple at all.

On that reading Cook did not copy Tenar. He took her apart. TENAR becomes RANET, but the woman guarding the sacred underground place is no longer necessarily the one who bears the reversed name. The title may keep a trace of her. Tenar is not one priestess among several but the One Priestess, the singular supreme figure of her place, and Cook writes not a giant snake but the giant snake-queen, with the definite article. That does not make the snake-queen a priestess. It is a reason not to read the word as decoration.

One correspondence needs holding at arm's length. Darkness is constitutive in Atuan, and the humans beneath the Forbidden City hate those who bring light. That is striking. But Cook puts the light-hating people and the temple in separate sentences and never joins them, and using Atuan to join them would supply exactly the connection the passage withholds. It is recorded here, and not used.

This is a reading to test, not a finding. What it would explain is how the resemblance and the reversal can both be real while the name tells so little: the name may be the part of Tenar that survived the taking apart.

## Other possible sources

Tenar is not the only material older than I1 that bears on the passage, and the second trail is a strong one.

Long before AD&D, English sources knew a goddess called Renenet. In E. A. Wallis Budge's edition of the Papyrus of Ani, first published in 1895, she stands in the judgement scene as a goddess of plenty and good fortune, associated with the rearing of children. The British Museum's 1904 guide to its third and fourth Egyptian rooms, credited to Budge and H. R. Hall, calls her the celestial nurse and says she is sometimes depicted as a snake-headed woman suckling a child. That combination matters. The near name and the female serpent are not similarities assembled from modern sources. They were already joined under the name Renenet in English more than seventy years before I1.

The same figure reached popular mythology. The Larousse Encyclopedia of Mythology, published in English by Paul Hamlyn in 1959, carries an Egyptian chapter by J. Viaud, issued on its own as Egyptian Mythology in 1965. Its Renenet presides over suckling and gives the child its name and fortune, and can appear as a woman, a snake-headed woman, a lioness-headed woman or a uraeus, the rearing cobra. She is the goddess Egyptology now usually calls Renenutet.

Deities & Demigods, published in 1980, shows that AD&D knew her too. Its Renenet looks like an ordinary woman, and her distinctive power is protective favour: whoever she looks on gains the luck of the gods, and thereafter always saves, always hits, and cannot be struck. That places her in the game's own surroundings before I1. It is not needed to show she was available, and it does not show that Cook took her from there.

None of this identifies Ranet. What it does is make the Egyptian trail independent of the Le Guin one. Renenet brings together a near name, a female serpent form and a beneficent, nurturing character. Cook's fragment has Ranet, a giant snake-queen and an unnamed kindly god, and keeps them apart. If Renenet contributed, Cook may have drawn on different parts of her separately: a name near Ranet's, a serpent form like the snake-queen's, a nurturing goodness like the kindly god's. Whether that resemblance reflects a source, a coincidence, or one ingredient mixed with others cannot now be recovered.

The two trails need not compete. Tenar offers an exact reversal and a pattern of relationships that recalls The Tombs of Atuan before the name is considered. Renenet offers a near name joined in English sources to a female serpent form. Either resemblance could be coincidence. Either could have contributed on its own. Cook could also have known both and combined them, and the surviving text cannot distinguish among those possibilities.

If both contributed, each supplies something the other does not. On that hypothesis, Tenar would help explain the structure and the reversed name, while Renenet would help explain the female serpent form. The result would belong to neither source. It would be Cook's recombination of them, and it would explain why the fragment does not look more like Tenar.

Cook has described working this way. Discussing a different project, the Known World he built with Tom Moldvay, he spoke of pouring material into the pot and stirring. And the Forbidden City itself draws on more than one source: Cook named Robert E. Howard's Red Nails as his model for the city, while its oriental dragon comes from the Fiend Folio. Neither fact establishes what happened with Ranet. Together they make blending a hypothesis consistent with how Cook is known to have worked, rather than a device invented to reconcile two explanations.

## What the module allows Ranet to be

The second way to answer the question is to ask the module directly. Here the answer is much smaller, and it can be made exact.

This is everything I1 establishes. Ranet is a name attached to a temple. Cook does not call Ranet a god, a goddess, a priestess, a queen, a monster or a person. The kindly god of darkness is plainly a god and plainly worshipped, and it has no name. The giant snake-queen guards the temple, and that is the only relationship Cook states between any of the three.

Everything else is absent. Nothing says the snake-queen is Ranet. Nothing says Ranet is the kindly god, or that the snake-queen serves it, or that the sewer humans worship Ranet or can reach the temple at all. The word queen does not settle it either. Cook uses it once elsewhere in I1, for the giant ants' queen, immobile near the far wall of the nest and tended by her workers. That gives the word a biological sense in this book, but the ant queen is guarded and the snake-queen guards, so it cannot simply be carried across.

That is the most the module will bear: three objects, one stated relationship, and no identities.

Several readings of the temple of Ranet remain open within it. Ranet may be a god, and the temple hers in the ordinary sense; that reading costs nothing linguistically and is the one the phrase invites, but nothing in I1 supports it beyond the possessive. Ranet may be a person bound to the place, a priestess or intermediary whose name the sanctuary came to carry, as the Tombs were bound to their One Priestess. Or Ranet may be the snake-queen herself: the temple of Ranet, with Ranet in it. None of these can be established from the text, and the most familiar, Ranet as a god, is an inference too.

## The adventure Cook left

The rules of 1981 widen those possibilities rather than narrowing them. A referee had several ways to make the fragment playable, and none is forced by it. The module itself says yuan-ti abominations may be wholly serpentine and are often confused with nagas, so a referee could keep the answer inside I1. The Monster Manual's guardian naga, lawful good and found in sacred places, offered a ready-made guardian of a temple. Its couatl, a good and intelligent jungle serpent that local people regard as divine, shows that a benevolent serpent could even be taken for a god. And Cook names an oriental dragon elsewhere in the module as exactly that, calling the bullywugs' "god" a pan lung, while here he writes giant snake-queen.

Later, someone did choose. In 1999 The Scarlet Brotherhood made Ranet a Suel goddess of fire, poisoned and supplanted by Pyremius. That is not evidence of what Cook meant in 1981, and it has not been used as such here. But it is instructive another way: a later writer read the name as the phrase invites, as a god's, and built on it. It is one answer to Cook's unfinished prompt, and it shows how much room the prompt left.

Cook offered the prompt to be developed. He describes these adventures as outlines for how particular areas or buildings might be expanded, and a name, a lost temple and a snake-queen are enough for that. The god may be Ranet. The queen may be Ranet. Ranet may be neither. Those are choices open to the referee, not distinctions the published adventure makes.

So the two answers to the question come back unequal. The name can plausibly be traced, and the ideas around it identified. What Cook wrote can be established exactly, and the choices open to his referee reconstructed. What cannot be recovered is the step that came between.

Perhaps David Cook could settle it. The text cannot. It may explain why Ranet looks like Tenar backwards, and the resemblance between the two situations may come to seem too strong to be coincidence. But if Cook took from Atuan, he changed what he took, dividing relationships that Le Guin had joined and leaving only an outline, and nothing that survives in I1 records how. Whether the reversed name stayed with the figure who answers to Tenar, passed to the temple, became a god's, belonged to the snake-queen, or meant something Cook never wrote down, the published adventure does not say.

The most secure finding in this investigation is five letters read backwards, and it concerns the name, not what the name belonged to. Tenar can become Ranet on the page without Tenar becoming Ranet in Greyhawk. And whatever Le Guin and the Egyptian sources explain about Cook, they explain nothing inside the setting. There is no Earthsea and no Egypt in the history of the Forbidden City.

That is the mystery of Ranet. It may never be known.

This was not a keyed encounter awaiting recovery. It was an adventure Cook left for someone else to write.

## Questions the text leaves open

*Some could still be answered by David Cook. Others belong to the referee, because the published module does not settle them.*

**For David Cook**

> Is Ranet Tenar, from The Tombs of Atuan, read backwards?

> If so, did the snake-queen take Tenar's part, leaving Ranet only Tenar's name?

> Did you mean Ranet as a god, a priestess, or the snake-queen herself?

> Did you intend the kindly god, the temple and the snake-queen to belong to one religion, or to be three separate things beneath the city?

**For the referee**

> Is the kindly god the sewer humans worship Ranet, or another power entirely?

> Is the snake-queen Ranet herself, the temple's guardian, or a creature that has made the temple its own?

> What does the fabulous lost temple hold, and who else in the city knows it is there?
