There’s a hill near me that I used to have to stop halfway up to catch my breath.

These days I can walk to the top without stopping. The change happened gradually, over many walks. I couldn’t have told you how much easier it was getting while it was happening, but I remember the first time I made it to the top in one go, and how good that felt.

Other people walk up that hill too. Some are quicker than me and some are slower, but I don’t know where they started. Watching them doesn’t tell me how far I’ve come. Remembering where I used to stop does.

Money changes just as gradually. A little less owed, a little more put aside, one month fewer that needs the overdraft. None of it feels like much at the time, but over a few years it adds up to a different position. Yet with money, we often look at what somebody else has instead of remembering where we started.

## What other people’s money can’t tell you

The trouble isn’t just that comparing yourself with other people can make you feel worse, though it often does. It’s that it can’t tell you what you need to know.

You can’t see what somebody was given. You can’t see what they owe, what they inherited, what their rent is, who helps them, what they gave up, or what they’re quietly not paying. You’re looking at an output with none of the inputs, and a number with no inputs can’t tell you whether you’re doing well or badly.

That doesn’t make other people’s numbers useless. Knowing the going rate for your job is worth something. But that’s information about the world. It isn’t a verdict on you, and used as one it can only change how you feel, which is a poor thing to act on.

Comparison with your own past has the opposite property. You know far more of the inputs, because you were there for them. You know what you earned, what was going out, what you were frightened of, and what you decided. It isn’t a perfect picture, but it’s the most complete one you’ll ever have of anybody.

## What I could only see afterwards

When I look back at the decisions that turned out to matter, almost none of them felt significant at the time.

Putting more into a pension felt like a subtraction. Stopping extra payments I’d been making on a loan felt like giving up on something. Putting a little money into investments each month didn’t seem to make much difference. It was money I couldn’t spend, and for a long time I couldn’t see what it was achieving.

None of those were dramatic. Not one of them produced a moment where anything visibly changed.

They didn’t make me wealthy, but between them they’re the reason I’ve been able to stop working earlier than I expected.

The decisions that felt big at the time are mostly not on that list. That’s the part worth taking from this: what mattered wasn’t identifiable while it was happening, and it was only visible looking backwards.

## The comparison that works

So the useful question isn’t whether you’re doing well. There’s nobody to answer that against.

It’s whether you’re doing better than you were, and it’s answerable, because you were there.

Are you borrowing less to finish the month than you were a year ago? Is there anything set aside now that wasn’t there then? Do you know something about your own money that you didn’t know last year? Did a decision you made turn out to be worth repeating?

Most of the evidence is already in your banking app or online banking. Look back at the same time last year. How much money did you have left just before payday? Were you using an overdraft or credit card to get through the month? How much did you owe on loans or credit cards? Did you have anything saved?

Say that a year ago you were £300 overdrawn by the last week of the month and had nothing put aside. Now you reach payday £50 in credit, with £400 saved. Your pay may have barely changed. That’s a £750 turnaround: £350 in your current account and £400 in savings. Nobody looking at you would see it, and you might not feel any better off. But you’re no longer finishing each month in debt.

Doing better doesn’t have to mean having more money. Owing less, or having more room to choose, can matter just as much.

## Where the analogy breaks

The hill is a fair comparison because nothing about me changed except practice. Life isn’t always that tidy.

Sometimes the honest answer is that you’re further back than you were, and the reason isn’t a decision you made. Illness, redundancy, caring for somebody, a landlord, a relationship ending. Comparing yourself to a past self who had more isn’t a measure of your judgement in those circumstances. It’s a measure of the circumstances.

Memory also flatters. The decisions you remember are the ones you can see the results of, and the ones that quietly did nothing tend not to come to mind at all. Looking back is useful, but it isn’t neutral, and it will make you look slightly wiser than you were.

And your past self can become a scoreboard too. If every year has to beat the last one, you’ve simply moved the same problem indoors. The comparison is meant to show you what’s changed and why, not to award you a mark.

## One thing to do

Think of one thing that’s different about your money compared with a year ago. Something you can point to: a debt that’s smaller, a bill you could pay without borrowing, money set aside that wasn’t there before. It can be small.

Then ask what made the difference. Some of it may be a decision you made. Some of it may be circumstances: a pay rise, a cost that went away, plain luck. Both count, but only one of them is yours to repeat.

Finally, write down where you are today, with the date: what you owe, what you’ve set aside, and how much is usually left just before payday. Three numbers. Put a reminder in your calendar for a year from now.

Next year you won’t be comparing yourself with a memory, but with a record: what changed, by how much, and at least some of why. And you’ll have measured yourself against the one person whose starting point you actually know.
