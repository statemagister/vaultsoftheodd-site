There is a hill near me that I used to have to stop halfway up.

I do not stop now. I did not notice the change happening, and there was no day on which it happened. The only reason I know is that I remember stopping, and I remember where.

Nobody else's walking tells me anything about that. Somebody faster might have been climbing hills for twenty years. Somebody slower might have started last week, or be carrying something I cannot see. The hill only means something measured against the person who used to stop on it.

Money works the same way. The difference is that with money we tend to measure ourselves against somebody else entirely.

## Why comparing with other people tells you nothing

It is not that comparison is unhealthy, though it often is. It is that the comparison contains no information.

You cannot see what somebody was given. You cannot see what they owe, what they inherited, what their rent is, who helps them, what they gave up, or what they are quietly not paying. You are looking at an output with none of the inputs, and a number with no inputs cannot tell you whether you are doing well or badly.

So the comparison cannot guide a decision. It can only produce a mood, and moods are expensive to act on.

Comparison with your own past has the opposite property. You know all the inputs, because you were there for them. You know what you earned, what was going out, what you were frightened of, and what you decided. That is a complete picture, and it is the only complete picture you will ever have of anybody.

## What I could only see afterwards

When I look back at the decisions that turned out to matter, almost none of them felt significant at the time.

Putting more into a pension felt like a subtraction. Stopping a payment I had been making voluntarily felt like giving up on something. Small amounts going out monthly into investments felt like nothing at all, for years, because that is exactly what they looked like on a statement.

None of those were dramatic. Not one of them produced a moment where anything visibly changed.

And between them they are the reason I am now in a position to stop working earlier than I expected. Not because I became wealthy, which I did not, but because the income those decisions eventually produced can replace the earnings I would be giving up.

The decisions that felt big at the time are mostly not on that list. That is the part worth taking from this: what mattered was not identifiable while it was happening, and it was only visible looking backwards.

## The comparison that works

So the useful question is not whether you are doing well. There is nobody to answer that against.

It is whether you are doing better than you were, and it is answerable, because you were there.

Are you borrowing less to finish the month than you were a year ago? Is there anything set aside now that was not there then? Do you know something about your own money that you did not know last year? Did a decision you made turn out to be worth repeating?

Those questions have answers. And they are the only questions in this whole subject where you already have all the evidence you need.

## Where the analogy breaks

The hill is a fair comparison because nothing about me changed except practice. Life is not always that tidy.

Sometimes the honest answer is that you are further back than you were, and the reason is not a decision you made. Illness, redundancy, caring for somebody, a landlord, a relationship ending. Comparing yourself to a past self who had more is not a measurement of your judgement in those circumstances, it is just a measurement of the circumstances.

The record also flatters. The decisions you remember are the ones you can see the results of, and the ones that quietly did nothing tend not to come to mind at all. Looking back is useful, but it is not neutral, and it will make you look slightly wiser than you were.

And your past self can become a scoreboard too. If every year has to beat the last one, you have simply moved the same problem indoors. The comparison is meant to tell you which decisions are worth repeating, not to award you a mark.

## One thing to do

Think of one thing you decided about money a year ago. Any decision, however small, including a decision not to do something.

Then ask what it actually produced. Not how it felt at the time, and not whether it was the cleverest thing you could have done. What is different now because of it.

That question does two things at once. It tells you whether that kind of decision is worth making again, which is the practical use comparison has. And it puts you in the habit of measuring against the one person whose starting point you actually know.
