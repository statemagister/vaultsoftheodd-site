You spend a working life being given one instruction about a pension, and it never changes.

Put money in. Take what your employer will add. Do not touch it. Every letter, every statement, every conversation, for decades, is a version of that.

Then one day you are old enough to take it, and the instruction runs out. Nobody has told you what happens next, because for thirty years there was nothing to tell.

What happens next is that the pot stops being one thing.

## Some of these doors close and some stay open

For all those years a pension is a single object. Money goes in, it grows or it does not, and there is a number on a statement.

When you can finally take it, you have to decide what you want the money to do. There is more than one answer.

Some of those decisions can be changed later. Some cannot.

That is the first thing worth knowing.

## What the pot can become

**An income promised for life.** You hand some or all of the pot to an insurer, and they pay you an income for as long as you live. That is called an annuity.

What it pays depends on choices you make when you buy it. Whether the income stays level or rises each year. Whether it carries on to a partner after you die. Whether it keeps paying for a set number of years even if you do not live that long. Adding those protections usually means starting with a lower income.

What you buy is certainty and the end of the question. But the pot is no longer yours to use differently.

That is the door that closes. There is a short cooling-off period, and after that the terms you chose are the terms you have.

**Money that stays invested while you take some of it.** The pot stays where it is, still rising and falling, and you take out what you choose when you choose. That is called drawdown. The money can keep growing, but it can also fall. You can change what you take, but there is no promise that it will last.

That door stays open. You can take more one year and less the next, stop entirely, or decide at seventy-five that you want the certainty after all and buy an annuity then. Money already taken out cannot simply be put back as though the withdrawal never happened.

**Cash.** You take some or all of it out as money. Simple, and the least forgiving, because tax applies to most of it and spent money does not come back.

You do not have to pick only one. You can take some as cash, put part into an annuity for a floor of guaranteed income, and leave the rest invested. A decision made now does not have to be the last one.

## The amount is your decision

Here is the thing that surprised me, and it is not on any statement.

In drawdown, you decide how much to take. Not the pension company, not a formula. You.

Apart from any tax-free part, that money counts as income alongside whatever else you have coming in. Take more and you may pay more tax.

So the amount matters, and it is one of the decisions that is yours to make.

## What I intend to do, which is not the same as what I have done

I have made my decision and I will not be acting on it for some years yet, so read this as a plan rather than a result.

I intend to leave mine invested and draw a modest amount each month. Not because drawdown is better, but because I have income from elsewhere already, and what I need from the pension is a small top-up rather than a floor to stand on. If I had nothing else coming in, that guaranteed income would look much more important.

I have chosen the amount deliberately rather than picked a round number, for the reason above.

Whether I take the tax-free portion up front is the part I keep turning over. My automatic assumption was that you take it, because it is tax free and it is there. But money taken out is no longer invested inside the pension, and if I would only put it somewhere else to do much the same job, the reason for moving it is less obvious than it first appears.

I may still take it. I have not decided, and I would rather say so than tidy it into a conclusion.

## Where this stops working

**Open and closed is only a way of sorting the choices.** It does not tell you which is right. That depends on what else you have coming in, whether anybody depends on you, your health, your savings and what you need the pension to do. Flexibility may matter a great deal if you have other income. A guaranteed floor may matter more if you do not.

**Some pensions are not this kind of pension at all.** If yours promises an income based on your salary and years of service, most of the above does not apply, and giving that promise up is a very different decision from choosing what to do with a pot. Find out which sort you have before reading anything else about options.

**Nobody knows how long they will live**, which is the uncomfortable fact under all of it. An annuity looks poor value if you die early and excellent value if you live to ninety-five. Drawdown is the reverse. There is no way to know which of those you will be.

**Taking money out can change what you are allowed to put in.** Taking money flexibly can reduce how much you are allowed to pay into a pension later. If you are still working and contributing, check before taking anything out.

**And this is where free, government-backed guidance genuinely exists.** It is worth using before making a decision you cannot reverse.

## One thing to do

Long before any of this, find out three things about the pension you already have.

What sort it is: a pot with a value, or a promise of an income based on your salary.

What age you can take it. It is not the same for everyone, and it is not the same as the State Pension age.

And whether there is anything attached to it that would be lost by moving it. Older pensions sometimes carry guarantees that are worth more than they look.

None of that commits you to anything. It just means that when the decision arrives, you are choosing rather than being presented with a form.
