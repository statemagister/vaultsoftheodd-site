Walk towards the horizon and it moves.

Not because it’s running away from you, and not because you’re walking too slowly. It moves because it was never a place you could reach. It’s a line defined by where you happen to be standing, so it changes with every step, and it will still be exactly as far away when you stop.

The idea of enough behaves like that, and it’s worth understanding why before deciding it says something unflattering about you.

## The question that cannot be answered

Ask yourself how much money would be enough and notice what the question actually requires.

Enough for how long. Growing at what rate. With prices rising by how much. Assuming what happens to your health, your work, your rent, the people who depend on you. Every one of those is unknowable, so the sum has no answer, and a question with no answer doesn’t sit quietly. It comes back.

What arrives instead of an answer is a feeling, and the feeling always does the same thing. It sits slightly above wherever you are.

That’s what makes it so demoralising. Save more and it moves. Earn more and it moves. The target that was going to let you stop worrying turns out to be attached to you, at a fixed distance, like the horizon.

And then a perfectly reasonable conclusion follows: that saving doesn’t work, because you’ve done it and nothing has changed.

## Change the unit

Here’s the thing that made the difference for me, and it wasn’t discipline.

I stopped asking how big the pot needed to be and started asking what income it would produce.

That sounds like a small change, and it isn’t, because one of those questions can be answered and the other can’t. A pot has to be measured against a lifetime you can’t see. An income can be measured against a month you already know very well, because you’ve been paying for it for years.

Say you have £60,000 put aside. Is that enough? There’s no way to tell. It depends on how long it has to last, what it earns and what prices do, and each of those is a guess.

Now ask what it pays. If it earned 4% a year, that’s £2,400 a year, or £200 a month. Your month costs, say, £1,500. So you have £200 coming in against £1,500 going out. You know exactly where you stand.

£200 isn’t £1,500, but it might already cover one bill. That’s a target you can reach. Then you can move on to the next bill, and maybe the one after that, until it’s all covered and then some.

Notice what’s happened to the difficult question. It’s gone.

You’re no longer asking whether a lifetime of savings will be enough. You’re asking whether one month’s income covers one month’s life. That’s a comparison, not a prediction, and it uses two numbers you already possess.

It’s also available to somebody who’s certain they’re bad with money, because once you know what it pays, there’s nothing to work out. It’s bigger or smaller.

## What I actually did

My own version was that the dividends from what I’d put aside should pay something like an ordinary salary. Not a comfortable one. An ordinary one.

It took a long time and I got there.

And I’m still not certain it’s enough.

I want to be honest about that rather than tidy it away, because tidying it away would make this article a lie. Writing the number down did the thing it promised: the target stopped moving, and I could tell whether I was above or below it. What it didn’t do was remove the uncertainty. It gave me a fixed point to be uncertain about, which is a real improvement and isn’t the same as peace of mind.

If somebody tells you that a written number will make you feel safe, be careful with the rest of what they’re selling.

## Where the analogy breaks

The horizon never moves closer. A number can.

Your first figure will probably be wrong. Mine was. That’s not a failure of the method. It’s the method. You write down a stake in the ground so that you have something to revise, rather than a feeling that revises itself without telling you.

An income target can also move on its own, because the income isn’t a promise. What pays you this year may pay less next year. Prices rise while the figure stays still. Neither of those means the exercise was pointless. They mean the number needs looking at occasionally rather than never.

And having a number isn’t the same as having the money. This article can’t help with the second thing. What it can do is stop the first thing from being impossible. A target you can see is the difference between saving towards something and simply not spending.

## One thing to do

Don’t start with the whole of your life. That’s the version of this question that has no answer.

Start with what your money does now. Work out what your savings or investments pay you in a month: take the interest or dividends from your last yearly statement and divide by twelve. If they don’t pay anything yet, write zero. That’s a starting point, not a failure.

Not what they’re worth. What they pay.

Then decide what you’d like that number to do first. It doesn’t have to be a bill. It might be a night out each month, or two, without having to worry so much about it. It might be your phone, or your energy. Or it might simply be knowing that what you’ve put aside is already working for you, even if it can’t cover everything yet.

Whatever you choose, give it a figure. Then you have two numbers you can compare: what your money pays, and what you want it to pay for. If the first is bigger, you’re there. If it isn’t, you know exactly how far away you are.

When you reach it, choose the next thing. One day it might be the whole month.
