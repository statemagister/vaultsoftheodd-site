You have cancelled two subscriptions and freed up about thirty pounds a month.

You also have a loan and three credit cards. You pay all four of them every month, on time, without fail. And the balances are still going up.

So the thirty pounds has two obvious homes. It could go into savings, where at least it would be yours. Or it could go at the debt, in which case the fair thing is presumably to split it four ways.

Both of those instincts are reasonable, and both are usually wrong.

## Why paying every month is not the same as paying it off

Start with why the balances are climbing while you are paying them.

A minimum payment is not designed to clear a debt. It is designed to keep the account in good standing, and on some cards it is barely more than the interest being added that month. Pay it, and almost all of it goes on the interest. What comes off the balance is the change left over.

Do that for a year on a card that is also being used occasionally, and the balance is higher in December than it was in January, even though you never missed a payment.

That is not a failure of discipline. It is what the arrangement does, and it is why the thirty pounds matters more than it looks.

## Why splitting it four ways does nothing

Thirty pounds divided by four is seven pounds fifty each.

On a card where the minimum is barely covering the interest, seven pounds fifty extra changes the direction of the balance by almost nothing. Do it to four debts at once and you get four almost-nothings. In a year you will have paid out three hundred and sixty pounds and everything will look roughly as it did.

The same thirty pounds aimed at one of them can finish it.

That is the whole difference. Spread money changes nothing you can see. Concentrated money removes a debt, and removing a debt does something that spreading never does.

## What clearing one hands back to you

When a debt disappears, so does its minimum payment.

That payment does not vanish into the air. It becomes yours again, every month, for as long as you live. And the money you can now aim at the next debt is the thirty pounds plus whatever that cleared debt was taking.

Then the next one goes faster. And when that clears, the money grows again.

This is why the order matters, and it is where the advice you will find may not fit your situation. Much of it says to attack the debt charging the highest interest first. That is correct if you are asking which order costs the least in total, and it is the right answer for somebody with room to breathe.

But if you are servicing six or seven payments and you have thirty pounds, the more useful question is different. Which of these can I actually finish, and what does it hand back when I do?

I did this myself, years ago, with more debts than I care to write down. The ones I cleared first were not the expensive ones. They were the ones I could finish in a few months rather than in years, and each one that went released its payment into the next. Getting six or seven down to five, then four, meant the money distributed better every time. The big expensive ones took me nearly four years, and I only reached them with the money that the small ones had handed back.

That is usually described as a motivational trick, a way of feeling like you are winning. It is not. It is a cash flow decision, and the money you get back is real.

## So save it or pay the debt?

On the arithmetic alone, paying down expensive debt beats saving, every time. A credit card almost certainly costs you more than a savings account pays you, so money sitting in one while the other charges you is money working against itself.

Real life adds one complication, and it is not a small one. If nothing at all is within reach, the next thing that breaks goes straight back onto a card and undoes months of work. A small amount kept accessible is not a failure of nerve. It is the thing that stops the cycle restarting.

So the honest answer is a first small reserve, then the debt, and both at once if that is what it takes for either to happen at all.

## Before any of this, one thing to check

Not every debt is the same species.

Rent and mortgage arrears, council tax, energy bills, court fines and anything with a court or a bailiff attached behave differently from a credit card. The consequences of falling behind are not extra interest. They can be losing your home, losing a supply, or enforcement.

If anything on your list is one of those, none of what is above applies yet. Those come first, and free help exists to deal with them.

## Where the analogy breaks

Concentrating money on one debt is not always the right move.

Some borrowing costs nothing for a while. If a card is genuinely charging nothing until a date next year, aiming spare money at it before then makes no sense, though it is worth knowing exactly when that ends and what happens after.

Some debts charge you for paying them off early. That is rare on cards and not rare on loans, so check before overpaying rather than after.

And if the total is beyond anything thirty pounds can touch, then this article is the wrong tool and a free debt adviser is the right one. Rearranging small amounts around an unmanageable total is a way of feeling busy while nothing improves.

## One thing to do before the next article

Write down what you owe, in one place, with two numbers beside each one. What it costs you a month, and what you would need to clear it.

Then ask the question this article is about: which of these could I actually finish, and what would it hand back to me when I did?

You are not committing to anything by knowing. Most people carrying several debts have never seen them all on one page, and the page is usually less frightening than the not knowing.
