I left university partway through, needing work quickly, and took the first job that would have me.

It didn’t pay much. I’d also come away with a student loan: a balance sitting there on a statement, growing gently, and behaving in every visible respect like a debt.

So I treated it like one. I could see the interest being added, and I didn’t like watching the balance grow. So I worked out how much extra I’d need to pay to start bringing it down, and set up a standing order. It wasn’t a large amount. It was more than I could comfortably spare, but it felt like the responsible thing to do.

I kept it up for a good while, and the balance came down noticeably. What I didn’t know was that the balance wasn’t what mattered.

## What I had got wrong

Some time later, when I needed to cut what I was spending just to afford to live, I read properly about how student loan repayments actually worked.

Two facts changed the picture.

The first is that money was already coming out of my wages for it, automatically, based on what I earned. The standing order was on top of that, not instead of it.

The second is the one that matters. The amount taken from my pay had nothing to do with the size of the balance. It was worked out from my earnings. Whether the balance was large or small, whether it was growing or shrinking, the deduction was the same.

So the extra I was sending each month didn’t reduce what was leaving my pay. It reduced a number on a statement, and the number on the statement wasn’t billing me for anything.

I stopped the standing order. I needed to cut back anyway, and now I knew it was the right thing to cut.

## The question that actually decides it

There’s one question underneath all of this, and it isn’t the obvious one.

It isn’t "how much do I owe" or "how fast is the interest growing". It’s this: **am I going to repay this in full before it’s written off?**

Because student loans are written off. After a set number of years, whatever is left stops existing, and the number depends on which plan you’re on.

If you will repay it in full, because your earnings are heading somewhere that will clear the whole thing with years to spare, then overpaying is a real decision with a real benefit. Money in early means less interest later, and you finish sooner.

If you won’t, because the deductions will run their course and the remainder will be written off anyway, then extra payments buy you nothing at all. You’re voluntarily paying down a balance that was going to disappear, and the money leaves your life for good.

Everything else is noise to the financial calculation. The size of the balance, how alarming the interest looks on the statement, how much you dislike owing money. None of it changes the answer to that one question.

## When my circumstances changed

Later I had to reduce my hours, and my earnings dropped below the point where anything is taken at all.

So the deductions stopped. Nothing came out, because nothing was due.

I looked at the whole thing again then, expecting something to have changed, and nothing had. The same question applied and the same answer came back. I didn’t restart the standing order, and I’ve been content since to let the balance run out its time and expire.

That isn’t resignation and it isn’t a clever trick. It’s the outcome of asking the right question and accepting the answer it gave, which happened to be different from the answer my instincts had produced when I was twenty-something and frightened of a big number.

## Where this stops working

This is the point where I have to be careful, because the wrong lesson is easy to take from it.

**It isn’t "never pay extra".** For somebody who will clear the balance well before it’s written off, usually a higher earner, overpaying can genuinely reduce what they hand over in total. The article that says nobody should ever overpay is as wrong as the one that says everybody should.

**Nobody can know this at twenty-one.** Which of those two people you turn out to be depends on a working life that hasn’t happened yet. Careers change, health changes, hours change. Mine did.

**Voluntary payments can’t be undone.** A payment you make on top of what comes out of your salary is a voluntary repayment, and a voluntary repayment is non-refundable. If it turns out you were never going to clear the balance, there’s no route back. That asymmetry is the reason to answer the question before paying rather than afterwards.

**But money taken wrongly can come back.** That’s a different thing entirely. If repayments were taken while you earned below the threshold, or on the wrong plan, or before you were due to start repaying at all, those are refundable and there’s a route to ask. Worth checking, because nobody tells you.

**One exception is worth knowing.** In Wales, part of a first maintenance loan can be cancelled once repayment has begun, which is a rare case where a small voluntary payment does change the number. If you’re funded by Wales, read that rule before deciding anything.

**This is how UK student loans work.** Repayments that come out of your pay according to what you earn, and a balance that’s written off after a set number of years, are features of the UK system. Student loans in other countries, including the US, can work very differently, and overpaying can make far more sense where the debt doesn’t go away on its own.

**And this isn’t about whether the balance feels bad.** It usually does. A large number attached to your name is unpleasant whatever the arithmetic says, and if paying it off would let you sleep, that’s a real thing to buy. Just know what you’re buying, and that it’s peace of mind rather than money.

## One thing to do

Before you send the Student Loans Company a penny extra, find out two things.

Which student loan plan you’re on, and when the balance is written off under it. The second follows from the first, and both are published.

Then look at what has actually come out of your pay so far, and ask whether that rate, continued for the years left before the write-off date, would clear what you owe.

If it clearly would, overpaying is worth considering properly. If it clearly wouldn’t, you’ve just saved yourself the money I spent finding this out the slow way.

If it’s genuinely too close to call, do nothing yet. The write-off date isn’t going anywhere, and you can decide again in five years with better information than either of us has now.
