I left university partway through, needing work quickly, and took the first job that would have me.

It did not pay much. What it did pay came with a balance attached, sitting there on a statement, growing gently, and behaving in every visible respect like a debt.

So I did what you do with a debt. I worked out the interest, worked out what I would need to pay to make the balance go down rather than up, and set up a standing order. It was not a large amount. It was more than I could comfortably spare, which is how I knew I was taking it seriously.

I kept it up for a good while before I found out it was doing almost nothing.

## What I had got wrong

Some time later, needing to understand where all my money was going, I read properly about how the thing actually worked.

Two facts changed the picture.

The first is that money was already coming out of my wages for it, automatically, based on what I earned. The standing order was on top of that, not instead of it.

The second is the one that matters. The amount taken from my pay had nothing to do with the size of the balance. It was worked out from my earnings. Whether the balance was large or small, whether it was growing or shrinking, the deduction was the same.

So the extra I was sending each month did not reduce what was leaving my pay. It reduced a number on a statement, and the number on the statement was not billing me for anything.

I stopped the standing order.

## The question that actually decides it

There is one question underneath all of this, and it is not the obvious one.

It is not "how much do I owe" or "how fast is the interest growing". It is this: **am I going to repay this in full before it is written off?**

Because these loans are written off. After a set number of years, whatever is left stops existing, and the number depends on which plan you are on.

If the answer is yes, if your earnings are heading somewhere that will clear the whole thing with years to spare, then overpaying is a real decision with a real benefit. Money in early means less interest later, and you finish sooner.

If the answer is no, if the deductions will run their course and the remainder will be written off anyway, then extra payments buy you nothing at all. You are voluntarily paying down a balance that was going to disappear, and the money leaves your life for good.

Everything else is noise to the financial calculation. The size of the balance, how alarming the interest looks on the statement, how much you dislike owing money. None of it changes the answer to that one question.

## When my circumstances changed

Later I had to reduce my hours, and my earnings dropped below the point where anything is taken at all.

So the deductions stopped. Nothing came out, because nothing was due.

I looked at the whole thing again then, expecting something to have changed, and nothing had. The same question applied and the same answer came back. I did not restart the standing order, and I have been content since to let the balance run out its time and expire.

That is not resignation and it is not a clever trick. It is the outcome of asking the right question and accepting the answer it gave, which happened to be different from the answer my instincts had produced when I was twenty-something and frightened of a big number.

## Where this stops working

This is the point where I have to be careful, because the wrong lesson is easy to take from it.

**It is not "never pay extra".** For somebody who will clear the balance well before it is written off, usually a higher earner, overpaying can genuinely reduce what they hand over in total. The article that says nobody should ever overpay is as wrong as the one that says everybody should.

**Nobody can know this at twenty-one.** Which of those two people you turn out to be depends on a working life that has not happened yet. Careers change, health changes, hours change. Mine did.

**Voluntary payments cannot be undone.** A payment you make on top of what comes out of your salary is a voluntary repayment, and a voluntary repayment is non-refundable. If it turns out you were never going to clear the balance, there is no route back. That asymmetry is the reason to answer the question before paying rather than afterwards.

**But money taken wrongly can come back.** That is a different thing entirely. If repayments were taken while you earned below the threshold, or on the wrong plan, or before you were due to start repaying at all, those are refundable and there is a route to ask. Worth checking, because nobody tells you.

**One exception is worth knowing.** In Wales, part of a first maintenance loan can be cancelled once repayment has begun, which is a rare case where a small voluntary payment does change the number. If you are funded by Wales, read that rule before deciding anything.

**And this is not about whether the balance feels bad.** It usually does. A large number attached to your name is unpleasant whatever the arithmetic says, and if paying it off would let you sleep, that is a real thing to buy. Just know what you are buying, and that it is peace of mind rather than money.

## One thing to do

Before you send them a penny extra, find out two things.

What plan you are on, and when the balance is written off under it. The second follows from the first, and both are published.

Then look at what has actually come out of your pay so far, and ask whether that rate, continued for the years left before the write-off date, would clear what you owe.

If it clearly would, overpaying is worth considering properly. If it clearly would not, you have just saved yourself the money I spent finding this out the slow way.

If it is genuinely too close to call, do nothing yet. The write-off date is not going anywhere, and you can decide again in five years with better information than either of us has now.
