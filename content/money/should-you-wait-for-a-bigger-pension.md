A season ticket always looks like a bargain. Divide it by the number of home games and each match costs less than paying on the gate.

But that sum assumes you go to every match. Miss enough of them and the cheaper ticket turns out to be the expensive one. The deal’s only good if you turn up.

Delaying your State Pension works the same way, except that the number of matches is how long you live.

## What delaying actually does

You don’t have to claim your State Pension when you reach State Pension age. If you don’t claim it, it’s deferred automatically. You don’t have to tell anyone.

For people reaching State Pension age on or after 6 April 2016, it goes up by 1% for every nine weeks you wait. That’s just under 5.8% for a full year. Wait two years and every payment is about 11.6% bigger, for the rest of your life.

That’s the part people quote, and it sounds like a good deal.

What it leaves out is that you weren’t being paid while you waited.

## The sum nobody shows you

Say your State Pension would be £12,000 a year, and you wait two years because you don’t need it yet.

You’ve given up £24,000.

In return, your pension is about £1,390 a year higher. So the question is how long £1,390 a year takes to make up £24,000, and the answer is a little over 17 years. Start claiming at 69 and you’re somewhere in your mid-eighties before the extra has paid back what you didn’t collect.

Live beyond that and you come out ahead. Die before it and you’ve had less in total than if you’d claimed on time.

That sum keeps everything flat to make the trade visible. Real pensions go up each year, and the extra you earn by waiting goes up with prices, which isn’t always the same rate as the pension itself. GOV.UK’s own estimate, which allows for that, is that it takes over 15 years to get back one year of deferred full State Pension, and around a year longer for each further year you wait.

So there isn’t one break-even age that works for everyone. But it’s always years, not months, and the longer you wait, the longer it takes.

## Taking some of it back

There’s a choice the headline figure hides.

When you finally claim, you can take up to 52 weeks of what you didn’t collect as a one-off payment, with no interest added. You can combine that with a higher weekly pension for any time you waited beyond it.

So in the same example, waiting two years doesn’t have to mean giving up £24,000. You could take a year’s pension as a lump sum and keep the increase for the other year, about 5.8%. You give up less, and you get less extra.

That’s a different decision, and it can be the more useful one, because it turns waiting into something you can partly undo.

## So who should wait?

If you’re still working, don’t need the income yet, and expect to live a long time, waiting can make sense.

There’s also a better reason than coming out ahead. A bigger State Pension pays for however long you live, so it gives you more guaranteed income if you live longer than you planned for. That’s insurance against a long life.

But insurance isn’t profit. If your health is poor, if your family history points the other way, or if you need the money now, giving up payments today for a gain that takes well over a decade to recover can be a bad bargain.

And not needing the money isn’t a reason to leave it unclaimed. You can claim it and save it.

## What I’d do

I’ve already retired early, and my State Pension age is still some years away.

When I reach it, I intend to claim. I wouldn’t delay it for a bigger payment later, because I’ve no guarantee I’ll live long enough to recover what I’d given up.

That doesn’t mean waiting is wrong for everybody. Someone expecting a long retirement might value the extra income in later life more than the payments they’d miss now.

But I’ve already made one decision about retirement based on wanting the time while I have it. I don’t see a reason to reverse that thinking when my State Pension becomes available.

## Where this stops working

**Nobody knows their own break-even.** Life expectancy figures describe groups of people, not you. They can help you think about the question, but they can’t answer it.

**Benefits can change everything.** You can’t build up any extra while you or your partner get Pension Credit, Universal Credit or certain other benefits, and a higher pension can reduce some of those benefits later. Pension Credit is the trap: the State Pension you haven’t claimed is still counted as income when it’s worked out. If you might be entitled to it, check before you wait, because waiting can reduce your Pension Credit while paying you nothing in the meantime.

**It’s taxable.** The State Pension counts as income. If you’re still working, waiting can mean less tax now. But a bigger pension later can mean more tax then. Arrears are different: the tax calculation looks at the years the payments were originally due. Compare what you’d actually keep.

**Don’t assume a partner inherits it.** Under the new State Pension, you shouldn’t assume the extra you’ve built up by waiting passes to a surviving partner. That makes it different from savings, which stay in your estate.

**The rules can change.** The government opened a call for evidence on deferral in October 2026. It doesn’t propose any changes, but it’s worth checking the current rules before you decide.

**Advice from America doesn’t carry over.** In the US, Social Security goes up by 8% for each year you delay beyond full retirement age, up to 70. That’s far more generous than the UK’s increase, which is why American advice leans so heavily towards waiting. It doesn’t transfer to a UK State Pension.

## One thing to do

Find out your State Pension age and check your forecast on GOV.UK. It takes a few minutes.

Then put two numbers side by side: what one year of that pension is worth, and how much more a year you’d get for waiting it out. Don’t stop at the percentage. Look at the money you’d go without, and how many years it would take to get it back.

A bigger payment sounds better. The question is what you give up to get it.
