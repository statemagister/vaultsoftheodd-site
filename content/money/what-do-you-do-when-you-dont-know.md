Somebody who cooks properly does not decide what to have for dinner at six o'clock.

They decided on Sunday, or they keep the same handful of things in the cupboard, or they simply have a Tuesday thing they do. Not because those meals are the best available. Because deciding at six, hungry, after work, with nothing in, is how you end up ordering something you did not really want.

The decision was not improved. It was moved to a moment when it could be made calmly, or removed entirely.

A great many money questions are like that. The honest answer is often that you do not have enough information to decide well, and no amount of thinking about it produces any more.

## The questions that cannot be answered

Is now a good time to invest? Nobody knows, including the people paid to have opinions about it, and anybody who tells you otherwise is describing a guess in a confident voice.

Should I put this payment back in or take it? That depends on things you cannot see yet.

Is this high or low? You will find out in about five years.

The trouble with unanswerable questions is not that you get them wrong. It is that they stop you doing anything at all. Waiting for a good moment feels like caution and works like paralysis, and the money sits there while the question stays unanswered because it was never answerable.

So the useful move is not to get better at answering. It is to have a default that does not require an answer.

## Two defaults that do not need you to be right

**Put in the same amount, on the same day, regardless.**

A fixed amount buys more of something when the price is down and less when it is up, which means the average price you end up paying works out lower than the average price over the period. That is arithmetic rather than cleverness.

But the arithmetic is not really the point. The point is that the question of when disappears. There is no good moment to identify, no decision to postpone, and no month where you decide to wait and see. The standing order does not have an opinion.

**Put back what it pays you, unless the money has a job.**

Something that pays you interest or dividends gives you a small decision every time it does. Take it, or leave it in.

The default is to leave it, because if the money has nothing else to do, putting it back means the next payment comes from a slightly larger base, and the one after that from larger still. Slowly and then less slowly.

The condition on the end matters as much as the rule. If that money is paying for something, it has a job, and the default does not apply.

## What that looked like over twenty-five years

I started with twenty-five pounds a month.

Not because it was a strategy. It was what I could manage without noticing, and I had read enough to know that waiting until I could do it properly meant not doing it. It felt insignificant, and for a long time it looked insignificant too.

It went up as my pay did. By the end it was seven hundred and fifty a month, which would have been an unimaginable number to the person who started, and got there in increments small enough that no single one felt like a decision.

Everything it paid out went back in. Every dividend, for twenty-five years, without my ever considering taking one, because none of it had another job to do.

That is the entire method. Two defaults, running for twenty-five years, removing the need to make repeated decisions about timing.

## And this September both of them change

The monthly investing stops, and so does the reinvesting, and neither is because they failed.

They stop because they finished. What they were building is now the thing I am going to live on, so the dividends start coming to me instead of going back in. Money that had no job for twenty-five years has one now, which is exactly the condition the default always carried.

Getting there was not tidy. To move towards holdings that paid a higher income, I sold some things at a loss, and the ones I bought have not done well since. Judged on what they are worth, that decision looks poor and still does.

But they pay considerably more than what they replaced, and by then income was the job the money needed to do. So I gave up capital I might have got back, and bought income I could rely on sooner. I would not call that a mistake, and I would not call it a success either. It was a trade, and I knew which side of it I was taking.

Which is the thing worth taking from it rather than the outcome. Whether something is the right investment depends on what you need it to do, and what you need it to do changes.

There is one more change, and it is the part I did not expect.

I will keep putting something in, smaller than before, and it is doing a different job. An income does not stay still on its own. Prices rise, so what it buys shrinks. Dividends get cut, which I have had happen. So putting a bit back in is no longer building anything. It is holding it level.

Same action, three different jobs across twenty-five years, and the only thing that changed each time was what the money was for.

## Where this stops working

**A default is not a prediction, and it is not free money either.** If you already have a lump sum, investing it at once has historically done better than feeding it in gradually, for the simple reason that more of it spends more time invested. Regular investing solves a different problem: most of us do not have the whole sum today, we have some of it every month, and putting a whole sum in at once is hard to do even when the arithmetic says you should.

**Reinvesting only makes sense while the money has no other job.** If it is paying your bills, take it. If you have expensive debt, that is a better use. The default is for money with nowhere else to be.

**Both defaults assume you are not going to need it.** Money you might want in two years should not be in something that can fall, whatever the standing order says.

**And a default is not permanent.** Mine ran for twenty-five years and then changed, twice, because my circumstances changed. The thing worth watching is not whether you are still doing it. It is whether it is still doing what you set it up to do.

## One thing to do

Pick the smallest amount you would not notice leaving.

Not the amount you think you should manage. The amount that will still be there in eight months, on a month when something goes wrong, because a default that stops is not a default.

Set it to go out on the day you are paid, so it leaves before you have started counting, and then stop deciding about it. That is the entire feature.

And if anything you hold pays you something, find out whether it is being reinvested or paid out, because that setting was chosen by somebody. It may have been you, years ago, and it may not have been anyone.
