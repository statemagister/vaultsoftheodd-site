Is now a good time to invest?

I couldn’t answer that question when I started twenty-five years ago. I still can’t answer it reliably today.

What I could do was put twenty-five pounds a month into stocks and shares. It was what I could afford, and I didn’t need to know what prices would do next to keep doing it.

That didn’t mean I’d made a good investment. It meant I’d found a way to make one decision without having to make it again every month.

Perhaps you’re thinking it’s already too late. You should’ve started years ago, and now there’s not enough time for it to make a difference.

You might be right that starting earlier would’ve helped. But you can’t go back and make that decision. What you can decide is whether doing something now would help, and what that something should be. It might not be investing at all.

## The questions that can’t be answered

The question I started with isn’t the only one like it. Is this a good price to buy at? You won’t know how it turns out until later. If my investments pay me something, should I put it back in or take it? That depends on things you can’t see yet either.

Nobody can know the answers in advance, including the people paid to have opinions about them, and anybody who claims certainty is describing a guess in a confident voice.

Some decisions are hard because you haven’t found something out yet. Those you can research. Others are hard because the answer doesn’t exist yet, and no amount of research will produce it.

The trouble with unanswerable questions isn’t that you get them wrong. It’s that they stop you doing anything at all. Waiting for a good moment feels like caution and works like paralysis, and the money sits there waiting for an answer that was never coming.

So the useful move isn’t to get better at answering. It’s to have a default that doesn’t require an answer, especially for a question that keeps coming back.

## Two defaults that don’t need you to be right

**Put in the same amount, on the same day, regardless.**

A fixed amount buys more when the price is down and less when it’s up, so over time the average you pay per share comes out at, or a little below, the average price on the days you bought. That’s arithmetic rather than cleverness.

But the arithmetic isn’t really the point. The point is that the question of when disappears. There’s no good moment to identify, no decision to postpone, and no month where you decide to wait and see. The standing order doesn’t have an opinion.

**Put back what it pays you, unless the money has a job.**

Some investments pay interest or dividends, and you may be able to have that money reinvested automatically. That means buying a little more, which can earn something itself. Over time, those extra earnings can build on one another. Slowly and then less slowly.

Not everything pays out. Some shares pay no dividends, and some funds reinvest their income for you. This default is for money you actually receive and don’t need for something else.

Your provider might already have a setting for what happens to that money. It’s worth checking whether it’s being reinvested, held as cash or paid out.

Both of these save you from answering the same question again and again. Neither tells you whether what you’re buying is any good, whether you can afford it, or whether it suits you. Those questions still need answering, at the start and whenever your circumstances change. And both only work while they match what the money is for.

## What that looked like over twenty-five years

I’d read enough to know that waiting until I could do it properly meant not doing it. So I started small, and it felt insignificant.

The twenty-five pounds went up as my pay did, in increments small enough that no single one felt like a decision.

Everything it paid out went back in. Every dividend, for twenty-five years, without my ever considering taking one, because none of it had another job to do.

What that gave me wasn’t a clever result. It was twenty-five years in which I never had to be right about timing, and never had to decide again each month whether this was the moment.

## When the money’s job changed

For twenty-five years, I’d been trying to build something. Eventually, I needed it to help me get by rather than keep growing. So instead of putting the dividends back in, I started taking them, and the monthly investing changed too.

The defaults hadn’t become wrong. They’d stopped being right for what I needed.

Changing them wasn’t tidy. To move towards holdings that paid a higher income, I sold some things at a loss. Judged only on what they were worth, that looked like a poor decision.

But they pay more than what they replaced, and by then income was the job the money needed to do. So I gave up capital I might have got back, and bought income I could rely on sooner. I wouldn’t call that a mistake, and I wouldn’t call it a success either. It was a trade, and I knew which side of it I was taking.

That’s the lesson, more than the outcome. Whether something is the right investment depends on what you need it to do, and what you need it to do changes.

There’s one more change, and it’s the part I didn’t expect.

I still put a little money back in, but it’s doing a different job. Prices rise, so the income buys less over time, and dividends can be cut. The amount I put back is small, and it won’t come close to offsetting inflation. But over time, it might help the income keep up with prices a little better.

Same action, three different jobs, and the only thing that changed each time was what the money was for.

This isn’t only about investing. There are plenty of decisions where you can’t know what will happen next. Having a sensible default means you don’t have to keep guessing. You just need to notice when the reasons for choosing it no longer hold.

## Where this stops working

**A default isn’t a prediction, and it isn’t free money either.** If you already have a lump sum, investing it at once has more often done better than feeding it in gradually, for the simple reason that more of it spends more time invested. Regular investing solves a different problem: most of us don’t have the whole sum today, we have some of it every month, and putting a whole sum in at once is hard to do even when the arithmetic says you should.

**Reinvesting only makes sense while the money has no other job.** If it’s paying your bills, take it. If you have expensive debt, that’s a better use. The default is for money with nowhere else to be. And if any of it is held as cash, check whether that cash earns interest, and at what rate.

**Both defaults assume you aren’t going to need it.** Money you might want in two years shouldn’t be in something that can fall, whatever the standing order says.

**And a default isn’t permanent.** Mine ran for twenty-five years before I adjusted it as my circumstances changed. The thing worth watching isn’t whether you’re still doing it. It’s whether it’s still doing what you set it up to do.

## One thing to do

Think of a money decision you keep putting off because you can’t know how it’ll turn out. Whether to start saving now or later, whether to switch something, or whether to pay more off a debt.

First ask whether there’s something you could actually find out. If there is, find it out.

If there isn’t, decide what you want the money to do and choose a default that fits your circumstances. That might mean putting something aside regularly, or deciding not to commit any money until you can afford to.

Write down why you chose it. When those reasons no longer hold, look at the default again.
