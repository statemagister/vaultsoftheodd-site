A label does nothing to what is inside a parcel.

The contents are identical before it goes on and after. What it changes is how everybody else handles the box between here and where it is going, and nothing else at all.

It is also specific about what it covers. Writing "fragile" on something is about handling, not about what was in the box when you sealed it. And if the courier changes what they do about that word, your contents have not changed. Only what happens to them has.

An ISA works much more like a label than like an account, and confusing the two is where the problem starts.

## It is not a thing you own

We say "I have an ISA" as though it were an object, a thing with properties of its own.

It is not. It is a status that gets applied to money you were going to hold anyway.

Inside a cash ISA is a savings account. Inside a stocks and shares ISA are investments, the same investments you could have bought without one. The ISA does not choose them, improve them, protect them from falling in value, or make a poor one into a good one. What it changes is how the tax system treats what happens inside it.

That is the whole of it. Not a better account. The same money or investments, treated differently by the tax system.

## Which is why it protects different things in different places

Because it is a label rather than a thing, what it covers depends on what is inside it.

In a cash ISA, what would otherwise happen is that the interest gets taxed. So that is what it stops.

In a stocks and shares ISA, what would otherwise happen is that dividends get taxed and gains get taxed when you sell. So that is what it stops there.

Those are different protections doing different jobs, and the word ISA is used for both, which is exactly how we end up assuming it protects everything inside it from everything.

It does not, and April 2027 brings a very clear demonstration of that.

## Why bother, then?

Because the protection is not only for this year's return.

On a small cash balance, the interest may already fall inside the amount you can receive each year without paying tax on it, so an ISA may be saving you nothing at all today. Balances grow though, and rates move, and circumstances change.

The larger reason is what happens to money left alone. Interest and dividends that are not taken away in tax stay where they are, and then go on to produce something themselves. Over enough years the ISA is protecting not only what your original money produces, but what those protected returns go on to produce after that.

Gains work slightly differently. They matter at the point you sell something or move it, because a gain made outside an ISA can be taxable then and a gain made inside one is not.

So the useful question is not whether you have an ISA. It is what tax you would face without one.

## What changes in April 2027

This part is dated on purpose, and it is the part to check rather than trust.

At the Autumn Budget in 2025, the government announced that from 6 April 2027 the amount you can put into a cash ISA each year drops to £12,000 for people under 65, while the overall ISA allowance stays at £20,000. People aged 65 and over keep the £20,000 cash limit, from the start of the tax year in which they turn 65.

The obvious response to that would be to hold your cash in a stocks and shares ISA instead, and simply leave it sitting there as cash. So a rule is coming to stop it.

From the same date, interest paid on cash held inside a stocks and shares ISA will be charged at twenty-two pence in every pound. HMRC calls it the 22% charge. Your provider pays it rather than you, so nothing will land on your doorstep. The money will just be less than it would have been.

If your money is actually invested in something, none of this touches it. It applies to cash sitting there as cash, and to nothing else.

There is a good deal of small print underneath that about which things count as cash and which do not, and it is on the government's own page rather than in this article. That small print is worth noticing for one reason, though. A simple thing does not need rules about what it applies to. A label does, and the length of the small print is what tells you which of the two you are dealing with.

And notice what all of that does to the name.

An ISA is described everywhere, including by the people selling them, as tax free. From April 2027 that will not be true of one specific thing in one specific place: cash sitting as cash inside a stocks and shares ISA. The name will still say tax free, and the thing will no longer quite be.

That is not a scandal, and a label picking up exceptions is not unusual. But every exception written into a rule has a cost that appears on no statement anywhere, which is that understanding the thing becomes something you need either the time or the money to do. That cost does not fall evenly, and it does not fall on the people best able to carry it.

Which is the argument for asking what yours actually protects rather than trusting what it is called.

One caveat. At the time of writing this is announced rather than finished. The consultation is under way and the regulations are due to be laid in the autumn. Check it before acting on it.

## What I found when I looked

I hold both kinds. A cash ISA holding money I might need, and a stocks and shares ISA holding things that pay me.

When this change was announced I went to look at what it meant for me, expecting to have to do something, and found the answer was nothing at all. My provider does not pay any interest on uninvested cash sitting in the stocks and shares ISA. A charge on interest I was never receiving is not a charge.

What was more useful was the question underneath it. If cash sitting in that account earns nothing, what is it doing there?

That is the thing the announcement made me check, and it had nothing to do with tax. I had been thinking of the ISA as a general protection, and it turns out to be a specific one, and the specific one only matters if the thing it protects is actually happening.

## What this is called

The word for what an ISA does is a wrapper. It goes around something else and changes how that thing is treated, which is why money is held "in" an ISA rather than "as" one.

The amount you are allowed to put in during a tax year is your allowance. It is per year and it does not roll over: an unused allowance is gone at the end of the tax year rather than saved up.

That is the vocabulary. Everything else about them can be asked in pounds.

## Where the analogy breaks

The label is a good picture and it has limits.

A label describes what is inside. An ISA does not. Within the rules it is indifferent to its contents, and two people with the same kind of ISA can be holding entirely different things.

The protection may also be worth nothing if there was nothing to protect. A wrapper is not automatically doing something for you, and whether it is depends on your own balance, rate and tax position rather than on having one.

And it does not protect the thing inside from itself. An investment held in an ISA can fall in value exactly as fast as one held anywhere else. Tax free losses are still losses.

Finally, the rules are the part that moves. The mechanism in this article is stable: a wrapper changes the tax treatment of what is inside it and nothing else. Which taxes, which limits, and which contents count as what, are decided by whoever is in office, and April 2027 is a demonstration of exactly that.

## One thing to do

Log in to whichever ISA you have and ask one question about it. What is inside this, and what tax would apply if the label were taken off?

Start with the contents rather than the total. If it is a stocks and shares ISA, is any of it sitting there as cash rather than invested in something? And if some of it is cash, what is your provider paying you on it? Mine pays nothing, and money earning nothing inside a wrapper designed to protect earnings is being protected from a tax it was never going to attract.

That question survives whatever happens to the rules, because it is not really a question about tax. It is a question about whether the thing you are protecting exists.
