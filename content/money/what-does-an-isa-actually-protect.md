Wrap something up and nobody can see what’s inside.

The contents haven’t changed. The wrapping doesn’t make them better or worse, and it won’t stop them breaking if the parcel gets dropped. It just keeps whoever is handling it from looking inside.

An ISA works the same way, and the one it keeps from looking inside is the taxman.

## It is not a thing you own

We say “I have an ISA” as though it were an object, a thing with properties of its own.

It isn’t. It’s a status that gets applied to money you were going to hold anyway.

Inside a cash ISA is a savings account. Inside a stocks and shares ISA are investments, the same investments you could have bought without one. The ISA doesn’t choose them, improve them, protect them from falling in value, or make a poor one into a good one. What it changes is how the tax system treats what happens inside it.

That’s the whole of it. Not a better account. The same money or investments, treated differently by the tax system.

## Which is why it protects different things in different places

Because it’s a wrapper rather than a thing, what it covers depends on what’s inside it.

In a cash ISA, what would otherwise happen is that the interest gets taxed. So that’s what it stops.

In a stocks and shares ISA, what would otherwise happen is that dividends get taxed and gains get taxed when you sell. So that’s what it stops there.

Those are different protections doing different jobs, and the word ISA is used for both, which is exactly how we end up assuming it protects everything inside it from everything.

It doesn’t, and April 2027 brings a very clear demonstration of that.

## Why bother, then?

Because the protection isn’t only for this year’s return.

On a small cash balance, the interest may already fall inside the amount you can receive each year without paying tax on it, so an ISA may be saving you nothing at all today. Balances grow though, and rates move, and circumstances change.

The bigger reason is what happens when you leave the money alone. Interest and dividends that aren’t taken away in tax stay where they are, and then go on to produce something themselves. Over enough years the ISA is protecting not only what your original money produces, but what those protected returns go on to produce after that.

Gains work slightly differently. They matter when you sell something or move it, because a gain made outside an ISA is potentially taxable but a gain made inside an ISA isn’t.

So the useful question isn’t whether you have an ISA. It’s what that ISA is actually protecting.

## What changes in April 2027

This part is dated on purpose, and it’s the part to check rather than trust.

At the Autumn Budget in 2025, the government announced changes that will take effect from 6 April 2027. The overall ISA allowance stays at £20,000, so under-65s can still put £20,000 into ISAs each year, but no more than £12,000 of it in cash. The rest has to go into another kind of ISA, such as stocks and shares. People aged 65 and over keep the £20,000 cash limit, from the start of the tax year in which they turn 65.

The obvious response would be to hold your cash in a stocks and shares ISA instead, and simply leave it sitting there as cash. So a rule is coming to stop that.

From the same date, interest paid on cash held inside a stocks and shares ISA will be charged at twenty-two pence in every pound. HMRC calls it the flat-rate charge. Your provider pays it rather than you, so nothing will land on your doorstep. The money will just be less than it would have been.

If your money is actually invested in something, none of this touches it. It applies to cash sitting there as cash, and nothing else.

There’s a good deal of small print underneath that about which things count as cash and which don’t, and it’s on the government’s own page rather than in this article. That small print’s worth noticing for one reason, though. A simple thing doesn’t need rules about what it applies to. A wrapper does, and the length of the small print is what tells you which of the two you are dealing with.

And notice what all of that does to the name.

An ISA is described everywhere, including by the people selling them, as tax free. From April 2027 that won’t be true of one specific thing in one specific place: cash sitting as cash inside a stocks and shares ISA. The name will still say tax free, and the thing will no longer quite be.

That isn’t a scandal, and a wrapper picking up exceptions isn’t unusual. But every exception written into a rule has a cost that appears on no statement anywhere, which is that understanding the thing becomes something you need either the time or the money to do. That cost doesn’t fall evenly, and it doesn’t fall on the people best able to carry it.

Which is the argument for asking what yours actually protects rather than trusting what it’s called.

## What I found when I looked

I hold both kinds. A cash ISA holding money I might need, and a stocks and shares ISA holding things that pay me.

When this change was announced I went to look at what it meant for me, expecting to have to do something, and found the answer was nothing at all. My provider doesn’t pay any interest on uninvested cash sitting in the stocks and shares ISA. A charge on interest I wasn’t receiving isn’t a charge.

What was more useful was the question underneath it. If cash sitting in that account earns nothing, what’s it doing there?

That’s the thing the announcement made me check, and it had nothing to do with tax. I’d been thinking of the ISA as a general protection, and it turns out to be a specific one. That specific protection only matters if the thing it protects is actually happening.

## What this is called

The word the industry uses for an ISA is a wrapper, for exactly the reason at the top of this article. It goes around something else and changes how the tax system treats it, without changing the thing itself. That’s why money is held “in” an ISA rather than “as” one.

The amount you are allowed to put in during a tax year is your allowance. It is per year and it doesn’t roll over: an unused allowance is gone at the end of the tax year rather than saved up.

That’s the vocabulary. Everything else about them can be asked in pounds.

## Where the analogy breaks

The wrapper is a good picture and it has limits.

The taxman knows the parcel’s there. Your ISA provider reports to HMRC on your account every year. Nothing is hidden. The rules simply say that what happens inside isn’t taxed.

The protection may also be worth nothing if there was nothing to protect. A wrapper isn’t automatically doing something for you, and whether it is depends on your own balance, rate and tax position rather than on having one.

And it doesn’t protect the thing inside from itself. An investment held in an ISA can fall in value exactly as fast as one held anywhere else. Tax free losses are still losses.

Finally, the rules are the part that moves. The mechanism in this article is stable: a wrapper changes the tax treatment of what’s inside it and nothing else. Which taxes, which limits, and which contents count as what, are decided by whoever’s in office, and April 2027 is a demonstration of exactly that.

## One thing to do

Next time you see an ISA described as tax free, look past the name and ask what’s actually inside it.

If it’s cash, the thing being protected is the interest. If it is investments, it’s the dividends and any gains. Then ask whether that protection matters to you.

That’s the useful question, because an ISA does not make the thing inside it better. It only changes what happens to the tax.
