There is a particular kind of arithmetic that stops working.

You add up what has to go out this month and you add up what is coming in, and the first number is bigger. Not by a little. You go through it again in case you have made a mistake, and you have not.

Most people meet that moment believing one thing: that the amount each company has asked for is the amount that has to be paid. It is on the statement. It is what the letter says. It is what leaves the account automatically each month.

That belief is what makes the situation feel closed.

## What a debt management company actually does

Years ago I was in that position, badly enough that I could not see a way out of it.

One of the lenders suggested I speak to a debt management company. So I looked into what they do. They contact everyone you owe, explain what you can realistically afford, propose smaller payments, and manage the arrangement afterwards. For that they take a percentage of what you pay each month.

Reading it through, one thought arrived and did not leave. That is a set of phone calls. I could make those phone calls.

I want to be careful here, because "do it yourself" is not the lesson. The lesson is the fee. There are free services that do exactly this work, properly and better, and there is no reason anyone should pay a percentage of their own money for it. If you would rather not make the calls, use one of them. What you should not do is assume the only options are paying in full or paying somebody to negotiate.

## The first attempt failed

I rang each company and asked what they would accept.

They told me. Each of them named a figure lower than the contractual payment, which felt like progress, and then I added the figures up.

It was still more than I had.

I tried it for a month anyway, because I could not think what else to do. It meant going without meals, and it meant watching the rent get harder. A month was enough to establish that it did not work.

Asking what they would accept had produced a set of answers that were each reasonable on their own and impossible together.

That is the flaw in the question. Every company answers for itself. Nobody is looking at the whole page except you.

## The second attempt

A month later I rang them all back, and this time I did not ask anything.

I told them. This is what I can afford to pay you, and it is about half what you asked for. I had worked it out first, from what actually came in and what actually had to go out, and I had divided what was left between them before I picked up the phone.

Some said yes straight away. Some asked whether I could manage a bit more. Some refused and said the arrangement was the arrangement.

And here is the part I had not anticipated. Because several of them accepted less, there was money spare against the ones who would not move, so I could pay those in full. The refusals stopped mattering, because the agreements had made room for them.

It took nearly four years on that schedule. As things slowly improved I paid more than I had promised, and it finished about six months early.

## What was actually happening

None of this was clever, and none of it was leverage.

A company deciding whether to accept less from you is comparing your offer with what they would otherwise get. Their alternatives are slow, expensive and uncertain. Chasing somebody who cannot pay, passing it to a collection agency for pennies, going to court, or watching the whole thing disappear in a bankruptcy.

Against those, a smaller payment that actually arrives every month is often the best available outcome for them. That is why the answer is sometimes yes, and it is why even the interest is occasionally negotiable.

It is worth being precise about this. That comparison is theirs to make, not yours to imply. Nobody should be threatening bankruptcy to extract a deal. You state what is true and affordable, and they decide whether it beats their alternative.

## Where this stops working

Not everything you owe behaves like a credit card.

Rent and mortgage arrears, council tax, energy debt and court fines can be enforced in ways ordinary borrowing cannot. Falling behind on those is not a matter of interest. It can mean losing your home or your supply. They come first, and this article is not the right tool for them.

Agreeing to pay less than the contractual amount is also recorded on your credit file, as an arrangement to pay, and it can stay there for up to six years. That matters if you expect to need a mortgage, a tenancy reference or a car on finance in the near future. It did not matter to me, because the alternative was worse, and for many people it is the same calculation. But it should be a decision rather than a surprise.

Some creditors say no. Mine did, and it worked anyway, but not everybody's arithmetic leaves room for that.

And if the total is beyond what any rearrangement can fix, the honest answer is not a better phone call. It is free advice from somebody who does this every day, and possibly a formal solution rather than an informal one.

## One thing to do before the next article

Before any phone call, work out your own number.

What actually comes in. What genuinely has to go out, with the debts that carry the most serious consequences dealt with first. Then whatever is left, divided between the rest.

That figure is the one you take into the conversation. Not what they asked for. Not what you think they might accept. What you can pay every month without ending up back here in six weeks.

You may find, as I did, that the number is smaller than anybody had asked for and still works. The arithmetic that stopped working is usually the wrong arithmetic.
