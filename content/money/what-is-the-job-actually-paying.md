Somebody once tried to hire me and I had decided to say no.

The salary was better than what I was on, but not by enough to move for. I liked where I was, I knew the people, and I had done the sum everybody does: their salary against my salary, a bit more in my favour, not worth uprooting a working life for.

Then they went through what came with it, and the answer changed in about four minutes.

Not because the salary went up. Because the salary turned out to be one item on a list, and the list contained things my job at the time did not have at all. Dental. Optical. Health cover. A scheme for buying shares in the company at a discount, and more shares on top of that.

I had been comparing my pay against their salary. Almost everything that made the offer worth taking was in the benefits, which I had never thought to look at.

## Your salary is not the whole of what a job pays you

The salary is the figure that gets advertised, discussed and negotiated, so it feels like the thing to compare. It is one part of what the job actually gives you. The other parts are worth money and nobody puts a price on them in the offer letter.

Some of them are worth a lot. What an employer adds to your pension is real money going somewhere you own, and the difference between one employer's contribution and another's can dwarf a difference in salary. Buying shares at a discount means paying less than other buyers for something you were free to buy anyway.

Some are easy to underrate in this country, because we have the NHS and think of health cover as something for people who do not. But the NHS does not do your dentistry or your eyes, and a package covering those is covering things you were paying for out of your own pocket.

And the list is longer than you might imagine. Life insurance. Accident cover. Travel assistance and emergency medical support. Maternity, paternity and adoption arrangements better than the legal minimum, which for anybody planning a family is worth more than a salary difference. Paid time off to care for someone, and to grieve, and unpaid time off without losing the job. An assistance programme you can ring when something has gone wrong. Learning you would otherwise pay for. Matched giving. Discounts on the things the company sells, which is worth a great deal if you were buying them anyway and nothing at all if you were not.

None of that appears on an offer letter as money, and it stays invisible until somebody tells you or you go looking.

Some of them are worth very little and sound impressive. Others are worth different amounts to different people: childcare help is transformative or irrelevant depending entirely on your life.

None of that is on the number.

## Which is why the same salary can be a rise or a cut

Once you are counting the whole thing, some ordinary situations invert.

The same salary with a shorter commute is a rise. You have bought back hours and stopped paying to travel.

The same salary with fewer hours is a rise per hour of your life, which may be the more useful unit, because the hours are the thing you cannot get more of.

The same salary with a better employer pension contribution is a rise, and a compounding one, though you will not see it in your bank account for decades.

And a higher salary that takes two hours a day and half your weekends can be a cut. Not metaphorically. Divide the whole package by the hours it genuinely consumes, including the travelling and the recovering, and the bigger number can lose.

## What I could not have asked about

The part that stayed with me is this. I did not find out about any of that by being clever. They told me.

If they had not, I would have turned the job down on the salary, and I would never have known what I had turned down, because you cannot ask about things you do not know exist.

Which cuts the other way too. I never went to my employer at the time and asked what they might do, or whether they had anything comparable. I assumed the salary was the offer, from both of them, and it was not from either.

## Where this stops working

**Not everything on a list is worth what it sounds like.** Some benefits are genuinely valuable, some are a discount on something you would never buy, and a long list is not the same as a good package. Count the ones you would actually use.

**Shares are pay you cannot spend yet.** Discounted or awarded shares usually have to be held for a period before they are properly yours, they can be taxed differently from salary, and their value moves. Leave at the wrong moment and some of it can simply not happen. It is worth knowing what the rules are before you count it as money.

**The value moves with your circumstances.** A package is not a fixed thing. Some of it scales with hours, some of it depends on being in a particular scheme or above a threshold, and a change in how you work can quietly change what you are getting. It is worth rechecking rather than assuming what was true when you joined is still true.

**And none of this decides whether to move.** Work is not only a payment. People stay for colleagues, for the shape of the day, for being good at something, and leave over things that no arithmetic reaches. This article is about knowing what you are comparing, not about what you should conclude.

## One thing to do

You do not need to be job hunting for this.

Find out what your current job includes beyond the salary. There will be a benefits page or a booklet, and most of us have never read ours properly. Look for the pension contribution and what it takes to get the maximum, and then anything you are entitled to and have never used.

Look for the things that match your own life rather than the impressive-sounding ones. Somebody planning a family and somebody planning to retire are looking at completely different halves of the same list.

Then, if you are ever comparing two jobs, put both whole packages side by side rather than two salaries. Add the hours, the travelling, and the money each one causes you to spend.

The salary is the number we naturally compare. It is rarely where the biggest differences are.
