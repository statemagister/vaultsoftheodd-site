Somebody once tried to hire me, and I’d already decided to say no.

The salary was better than what I was on, but not by enough to move. I liked where I was, I knew the people, and I’d done the sum everybody does: their salary against my salary, a bit more in my favour, not worth uprooting a working life for.

Then they went through what came with it, and the answer changed in about four minutes.

Not because the salary went up. Because the salary turned out to be one item on a list, and the list contained things my job at the time didn’t have at all. Dental. Optical. Health cover. A scheme for buying shares in the company at a discount, and more shares on top of that.

I’d been comparing my pay against their salary. Almost everything that made the offer worth taking was in the benefits, which I’d not thought to look at.

## Your salary isn’t the whole of what a job pays you

The salary is the figure that gets advertised, discussed and negotiated, so it feels like the thing to compare. It’s one part of what the job actually gives you. The other parts are worth money and they’re rarely given a value in the offer letter.

Some of them are worth a lot. What an employer adds to your pension is real money going somewhere you own, and the difference between one employer’s contribution and another’s can dwarf a difference in salary. Buying shares at a discount means paying less than other buyers for something you were free to buy anyway.

Some are easy to underrate, especially in the UK, because we have the NHS and think of health cover as something people elsewhere need. But there are exceptions: NHS dentistry can involve charges and be very hard to access, while many adults also pay for eye tests or glasses. A package covering those costs is covering things you might otherwise pay for yourself.

And the list is longer than you might imagine. Life insurance. Accident cover. Travel assistance and global emergency medical support. Maternity, paternity and adoption arrangements better than the legal minimum, which for anybody planning a family can be worth more than a salary difference. Paid time off to care for someone, and to grieve, and unpaid time off without losing the job. An assistance programme you can ring when something has gone wrong. Learning you would otherwise pay for. Matched giving. Discounts on the things the company sells, which is worth a great deal if you were buying them anyway and nothing at all if you weren’t.

Some of them are worth very little and sound impressive. Others are worth different amounts to different people: childcare help is transformative or irrelevant depending entirely on your life.

Not every job comes with much of this. For many, the offer really is the salary and the legal minimum. Employers that do offer more may use it as part of their pitch, as mine did. But it isn’t always written down as money, so it’s worth asking what comes with a job even when nobody mentions it.

## Which is why the same salary can be a rise or a cut

Once you’re counting the whole thing, some ordinary situations invert.

Keep the salary the same and change one thing, and it can become a rise. A shorter commute buys back hours and stops you paying to travel. Fewer hours is a rise per hour of your life, which may be the more useful unit, because the hours are the thing you can’t get more of. A better employer pension contribution counts too, even though the extra money goes into your pension rather than your bank account.

And a higher salary that takes two hours a day and half your weekends can be a cut. Not metaphorically. Divide the whole package by the hours it genuinely consumes, including the travelling and the recovering, and the bigger number can lose.

## What I didn’t know to ask about

The part that stayed with me is this. I didn’t find out about any of that by being clever. They told me.

If they hadn’t, I would have turned the job down on the salary, and I would never have known what I’d turned down, because I didn’t know to ask.

Which cuts the other way too. I never went to my employer at the time and asked what they might do, or whether they had anything comparable. I assumed the salary was the offer, from both of them, and it wasn’t from either.

## Where this stops working

**Not everything on a list is worth what it sounds like.** Some benefits are genuinely valuable, some are a discount on something you would never buy, and a long list isn’t the same as a good package. Count the ones you would actually use.

**Sometimes it really is the salary.** If the extras don’t matter to you, because you’d never use them or already have them elsewhere, then the money is the thing to compare. And the salary may be negotiable, so it’s worth asking whether there’s room for more rather than treating the first figure as fixed.

**Shares come with conditions.** Discounted shares and share awards aren’t all the same. Some are yours when you buy them, while others only become yours if you’re still employed on a set date. Their value can fall, and the tax treatment depends on the scheme. Find out which rules apply before treating them as part of your pay.

**The value moves with your circumstances.** A package isn’t a fixed thing. Some of it scales with hours, some of it depends on being in a particular scheme or above a threshold, and a change in how you work can quietly change what you’re getting. It’s worth rechecking rather than assuming what was true when you joined is still true.

**And none of this decides whether to move.** Work isn’t only a payment. People stay for colleagues, for the shape of the day, for being good at something, and leave over things that no arithmetic reaches. This article is about knowing what you’re comparing, not about what you should conclude.

## One thing to do

You don’t need to be job hunting for this.

Find out what your current job includes beyond the salary. There may be a benefits page or a handbook, and many of us have never read ours properly. Look for the pension contribution and what it takes to get the maximum, and then anything you’re entitled to and have never used.

Look for the things that match your own life rather than the impressive-sounding ones. Somebody planning a family and somebody planning to retire are looking at completely different halves of the same list.

Then, if you’re ever comparing two jobs, put both whole packages side by side rather than two salaries. Add the hours, the travelling, and the money each one causes you to spend.

The salary is the number we naturally compare. It isn’t always where the biggest differences are.
