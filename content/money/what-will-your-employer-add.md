You know the moment when somebody says they will give you a hand with the wardrobe.

They are not going to carry it up the stairs by themselves while you watch. The offer only becomes real when you pick up your end. Stand there with your hands in your pockets and the wardrobe stays exactly where it is, along with the help that was genuinely on offer.

Some of what your job pays you works like that. It is attached to your employment, it is real, and it only comes out once you have put something down yourself.

Almost nobody knows how much theirs is.

## Money that is already moving

If you are employed, aged 22 or over and earning more than £10,000 a year, some of your pay is almost certainly going into a pension already, and your employer is putting something in alongside it. That happens automatically. You did not have to ask.

Those two figures are for the 2026/27 year and they get reviewed annually, so check yours rather than assuming. If you are younger than 22 or earning less than that, you can usually still ask to join, and it is worth asking, because the employer's money normally comes with you.

What most people do not know is where the ceiling is.

Many employers will add more than the minimum they are obliged to add, but only if you increase your own. Put in a little more and they put in a little more. Stop, and they stop. It varies enormously between employers, and the only way to find out is to look.

## This is not free money, and it does not need to be

The pitch you usually hear is that this is free money. It is not, and overselling it does nobody any favours.

It is money you cannot touch for a very long time, in exchange for money you could have used this month. That is a real cost, and for somebody whose month does not currently reach payday it is a serious one.

What is true is narrower and much harder to argue with. If your employer is willing to add money and you have not gone as far as they will go, then part of what your job offers you is sitting there unclaimed. Few other ordinary financial decisions begin with somebody else adding money to yours.

That is worth knowing even if you decide to do nothing about it this year.

## What it looked like for me

My employer matched what I put in, and the preset was not the ceiling.

I found that out on day one. I knew there would be a portal, I assumed there would be a ceiling somewhere, and I sat and moved my own contribution up and down until I found the point where their side stopped moving with it. Ten minutes, sitting at a desk. I set it there and left it.

One screen further in was another default, this time for what the pension bought with the money. That is a different decision and I come back to it in a later article.

Plenty of people I worked alongside never touched theirs.

Not because they had thought about it and decided against it. The preset number was already there when they joined, somebody else had chosen it, and nobody had ever told them there was anything to find. That is the only real difference between us. I knew to look.

That is the whole reason this article exists. The default is a decision, and it was made by somebody who has never met you.

## What this is called

You and your employer put money aside for your life after work, and it is held somewhere separate from your bank account. That place is your workplace pension.

The money your employer puts in is called an employer contribution. It is not a bonus and it is not a favour. It is part of the package, in the same way your holiday allowance is part of the package.

That is all the vocabulary this article needs. How the money actually leaves your pay, and how tax is handled on the way, turns out to be three different arrangements with three different sets of rules, and it deserves an article of its own rather than a paragraph here.

## Where the analogy breaks

The wardrobe gets carried today. A pension does not.

Money in a pension is normally locked away until you are 55, and that rises to 57 in April 2028. It can help with a life years from now. It cannot pay an urgent dental bill next month, and it will not cover a difficult week. If nothing at all is set aside for the ordinary disasters, money you cannot reach is the wrong place for your next spare pound, and Why Leave Money Doing Nothing? is the one to read first.

If you are dealing with debt you cannot manage, the arithmetic can also come out differently, and that is a conversation to have with a free debt adviser rather than with an article.

Nothing here is a reason to go and change what your pension is invested in. A default fund is chosen to be a reasonable answer for somebody the scheme knows nothing about, which is not the same as being the wrong answer, and it is a sensible place to leave money. That decision has an article of its own.

Your employer's help has an edge to it too. Most will match you only up to a point, and above that you are on your own. Contributing more than that point can still be a reasonable thing to do, but you should know you are doing it without company.

And the offer is not quite as conditional as the picture suggests. Because of how automatic enrolment works, your employer's minimum contribution is already happening unless you actively opted out, so it is not that nothing arrives until you act. What is true is that opting out forfeits it entirely, and that anything above the minimum usually has to be triggered by you.

Finally, a pension is not one thing. Two people using the same word can be describing quite different arrangements with different rules, and I will get to that.

## One thing to do before the next article

No arithmetic this time. Three facts, and they are all on your latest payslip or your employer's benefits page.

What are you putting into your pension. What is your employer adding. And would your employer add more if you increased yours.

That third one is the only one that takes any effort, and it is usually a single line in a benefits document or a two sentence question to whoever runs payroll.

You are not committing to anything by finding out. This is the one question in the series where the answer might turn out to be that somebody has been holding a door open for you for years.
