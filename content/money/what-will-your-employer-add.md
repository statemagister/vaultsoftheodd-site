You know the moment when somebody offers to help you move some furniture, like a wardrobe.

They aren't going to carry it up the stairs by themselves while you watch. The offer only becomes real when you take part by picking up your end. Stand there with your hands in your pockets and the wardrobe stays exactly where it is, along with the help that was genuinely on offer.

Some of what your job pays you works like that. It's attached to your employment, it's real, and it only comes out once you've put something down yourself.

Not everyone finds out how much theirs is.

## Money that's already moving

If you're employed, aged 22 or over but under State Pension age, and earning more than £10,000 a year, some of your pay should already be going into a pension, and your employer should be putting something in alongside it. That happens automatically unless you've opted out. You didn't have to ask.

These earnings thresholds are reviewed every year. If you're aged 16 to 74 and haven't been enrolled automatically, you can usually still ask to join. If you earn more than £6,240 a year, your employer has to pay in alongside you. These figures are for the 2026/27 tax year.

What's easy to miss is where the ceiling is.

Some employers will add more than the minimum they have to, but only if you increase your own. Put in a little more and they put in a little more, up to a limit. It varies between employers, and the only way to find out is to look.

## This isn't free money, and it doesn't need to be

The pitch you usually hear is that this is free money. It isn't, and overselling it does nobody any favours.

It's money you can't touch for a very long time, in exchange for money you could have used this month. That's a real cost, and for somebody whose month doesn't currently reach payday it's a serious one.

What's true is narrower and much harder to argue with. If your employer is willing to add money and you haven't gone as far as they'll go, then part of what your job offers you is sitting there unclaimed. Few other ordinary financial decisions begin with somebody else adding money to yours.

That's worth knowing even if you decide to do nothing about it this year.

## What it looked like for me

My employer matched what I put in, and the preset wasn't the ceiling.

I found that out on day one. I knew there would be a portal, I assumed there'd be a ceiling somewhere, and I sat and moved my own contribution up and down until I found the point where their side stopped moving with it. Ten minutes, sitting at a desk. I set it there and left it.

One screen further in was another default, this time for what the pension bought with the money. That's a different decision.

Plenty of people I worked alongside never changed their contribution.

The preset number was already there when they joined, and somebody else had chosen it. I told as many of them as I could that there was more on offer. Some increased their contributions. Many more didn't, and they may well have had their reasons, including the one above: money you can't touch for decades is a real cost when this month is already tight.

That's the whole reason this article exists. The default is a decision, and it was made by somebody who's never met you.

## What this is called

Your workplace pension is a way of putting money aside for your life after work, with your employer contributing too.

The money your employer puts in is called an employer contribution. It isn't a bonus and it isn't a favour. It's part of the package, in the same way your holiday allowance is part of the package.

That's all the vocabulary this article needs. How the money actually leaves your pay, and how tax is handled on the way, turns out to be three different arrangements with three different sets of rules, and that needs more room than a paragraph here.

## Where the analogy breaks

The wardrobe gets carried today. A pension doesn't.

Money in a pension is normally locked away until you're 55, and that rises to 57 in April 2028. It can help with a life years from now. It can't pay an urgent dental bill next month, and it won't cover a difficult week. If nothing at all is set aside for the ordinary disasters, money you can't reach is the wrong place for your next spare pound, and Why Leave Money Doing Nothing? is the one to read first.

If you're dealing with debt you can't manage, the arithmetic can also come out differently, and that's a conversation to have with a free debt adviser rather than with an article.

Nothing here is a reason to go and change what your pension is invested in. A default fund is chosen to be a reasonable answer for somebody the scheme knows nothing about, which isn't the same as being the wrong answer, and it can be a sensible place to leave money.

Your employer's help has an edge to it too. A match usually stops at some point, and above that you're on your own. Contributing more than that can still be a reasonable thing to do, but you should know you're doing it without company.

And the offer isn't quite as conditional as the picture suggests. If you were enrolled automatically, your employer's minimum contribution is already happening unless you actively opted out, so it isn't that nothing arrives until you act. What's true is that opting out forfeits it entirely, and that anything above the minimum usually has to be triggered by you.

Finally, a pension isn't one thing. Two people using the same word can be describing quite different arrangements with different rules.

## One thing to do

No arithmetic this time. Three facts, and they're all on your latest payslip or your employer's benefits page.

What are you putting into your pension? What's your employer adding? And would your employer add more if you increased yours?

That third one is the only one that takes any effort, and it's usually a single line in a benefits document or a two-sentence question to whoever runs payroll.

You aren't committing to anything by finding out. This is the one question in the series where the answer might turn out to be that somebody's been holding a door open for you for years.
