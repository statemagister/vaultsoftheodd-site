My cash reserve has been in two different accounts. I chose the first because it paid well, but the interest rate wasn’t why I moved it.

A reserve is money I keep aside for unexpected expenses, so I need to be able to get to it when something goes wrong. That limits where I’m prepared to keep it.

It started in an instant access savings account in my banking app, where I could reach it from my phone. Later, I moved it into an instant access cash ISA. I’ll cover ISAs separately in a later article.

So how much interest is worth giving up to make sure you can get to your money when you need it?

## What the higher number actually costs

An account that pays more but locks the money away for a year, or makes you give notice before you can take it out, isn’t a better version of the same thing. It’s a different thing, and it’s the wrong thing for this money.

Look at what you are trading. You are giving up being able to reach the money on the day the car fails its MOT, in exchange for a little more while nothing goes wrong.

That’s a bad trade, and it’s an easy one to make, because only one side of it is advertised.

The rate matters much more later, with money that genuinely will not be needed for years. Here, reaching it is the feature you are paying for.

Keeping a reserve accessible does not mean accepting almost no interest, though. The difference between savings rates can be substantial once you have built up a reserve. On £5,000, the difference between earning 1% and 4% is £150 a year before any tax. On £10,000, it is £300.

Check the withdrawal conditions as well as the advertised rate. Some accounts limit how often you can withdraw money, or reduce the interest rate after a certain number of withdrawals. That makes them less useful for a reserve you may need to reach more than once.

Check whether the advertised rate includes a temporary bonus, too. An attractive rate today may be considerably less attractive when the bonus expires.

## The other thing worth checking

Money held with an eligible UK bank, building society or credit union is normally protected by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS), up to £120,000 per person, per authorised firm.

The distinction is between the banking brand and the firm behind it. Two banks with different names can share the same authorisation, in which case your balances with both count towards a single protection limit.

You can check this using the FSCS bank and savings protection checker, linked below. Enter your providers and the amounts you hold with them to see whether their protection is shared. The £120,000 limit has applied since 1 December 2025.

For a first reserve this is comfortably academic. It is worth knowing anyway, because it is the one thing to check if you ever hold a large amount in one place, and because it is what makes "somewhere safe" a real category rather than a phrase.

## Where this stops working

Insisting that money must be instantly reachable is right for a reserve and wrong for everything else. Money you will not touch for ten years does not need to be available on a Tuesday, and keeping it that way has a cost.

A better rate isn’t a trick, either. It’s a real thing worth having, as soon as the money it applies to is money that can genuinely be left alone.

And an account paying nothing isn’t the safe choice merely because it’s the familiar one. Leaving money where it earns nothing is a decision too, and it quietly costs you as prices rise.

## One thing to do before the next article

Look at the interest rate on the account where you currently keep your spare money. Then compare it with an easy-access savings account.

Check three things: how much interest it pays, including whether the rate contains a temporary bonus; whether you can withdraw money whenever you need it without losing interest; and whether it shares its deposit protection with a bank you already use.

You may find that the account you have is perfectly suitable. If it isn’t, you now know what to look for in another one.
