The account my reserve sits in is not the one paying the most, and it is still smaller than I want it to be. Both of those are true.

It started as an instant access savings account in my banking app, picked because it paid well and I could reach it from my phone. Later, as retiring started to feel like a real date rather than an idea, I moved it into a cash ISA. Not for the rate. My income will not be big enough to use a full ISA allowance every year, so it makes more sense to split what I do use between cash and investments than to leave the cash side of it unused.

If ISAs and allowances are not something you have had to think about yet, ignore that part. It has an article of its own later on.

The thing I did not change was being able to get at it. That was never up for discussion, and it is the part worth explaining.

Which brings up the obvious objection.

If the money is only sitting there waiting for something to go wrong, why not put it wherever it earns the most?

## Because the job comes first

This money has one thing it has to do. It has to be there on the day something goes wrong.

Not next week. Not after a notice period. Not once you have filled in a form and waited. That day, or near enough that you are not borrowing while you wait.

Everything else about the account comes second to that, and once you accept it, most of the choosing becomes simple.

## What this is called

A bank can hold this money separately from the account you use every day, and it may add a little in return for being allowed to use yours while it sits there.

The separate place is called a savings account. The little it adds is called interest.

An account you can take money out of without waiting out a notice period is usually described as easy access. That is the phrase to look for on the page.

## What the higher number actually costs

An account that pays more but locks the money away for a year is not a better version of the same thing. It is a different thing, and it is the wrong thing for this money.

Look at what you are trading. You are giving up being able to reach the money on the day the car fails its MOT, in exchange for a little more while nothing goes wrong.

That is a bad trade, and it is an easy one to make, because only one side of it is advertised.

The rate matters much more later, with money that genuinely will not be needed for years. Here, reaching it is the feature you are paying for.

Check the small print either way. Some accounts limit how many withdrawals you can make in a year, and some quietly pay less for the rest of the year once you make one.

## The other thing worth checking

Money held with a bank, building society or credit union authorised in the UK is protected up to £120,000, for each person, with each separately authorised firm.

The scheme behind that has a long name, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, and you do not need to remember the initials.

The useful part is that two different brand names do not always mean two separate lots of protection. Several brands can belong to the same authorised firm and share one limit between them.

For a first reserve this is comfortably academic. It is worth knowing anyway, because it is the one thing to check if you ever hold a large amount in one place, and because it is what makes "somewhere safe" a real category rather than a phrase.

## Where the analogy breaks

Every article in this series ends by showing where its picture stops working.

Insisting that money must be instantly reachable is right for a reserve and wrong for everything else. Money you will not touch for ten years does not need to be available on a Tuesday, and keeping it that way has a cost.

A better rate is not a trick, either. It is a real thing worth having, as soon as the money it applies to is money that can genuinely be left alone.

And an account paying nothing is not the safe choice merely because it is the familiar one. Leaving money where it earns nothing is a decision too, and it quietly costs you as prices rise.

## One thing to do before the next article

Find out what your everyday account pays you on the money sitting in it. For most people the answer is nothing, and seeing that is usually enough.

Then look at one easy access account beside it. Not to chase the best rate in the country, and not to move anything you might need this week.

If the difference turns out to be a few pounds a year, take the few pounds and stop there. You are not trying to win. You are trying to make sure that the money is somewhere it can still be reached on the worst day of the month.
