Some months go wrong for no reason you can immediately see.

You didn’t buy anything you regret. Nothing broke, nobody was ill, there was no disaster. And yet you spent the last few days worrying about money. Something got paid late, or was missed, and you finished the month worse off than when you started.

The instinct afterwards is to think that you overspent or weren’t paying attention. Occasionally that’s true. Much more often, the problem was not how much you spent. It was when the money came in and went out.

## Three ways it has happened to me

I went on holiday and forgot about a payment I had set up shortly before. It was new, so I wasn’t used to allowing for it. It went out while I was away, and by the time I got home the month had already gone wrong.

Another time my pay arrived on the wrong side of a large bill. Nothing had changed about the amount I was paid or the amount of the bill. The dates simply fell differently from the way I was expecting, and I had assumed I had another week when I didn’t.

The third one is the one I think about most, because both decisions were sensible.

I had set up a credit card with a direct debit to clear the balance in full every month, which is exactly what you are supposed to do. Later I was offered twenty-four months at no interest on that card, and took it, which was also a reasonable thing to do.

What I didn’t do was go back and change the direct debit.

The card was still set to take the whole balance. But the balance was now a large one that I had deliberately arranged not to pay off quickly, and I didn’t have enough money in my bank account to cover it. What followed was a series of charges and problems that cost real money to sort out.

I hadn’t been extravagant. I had made two sensible decisions, months apart, without noticing that the second one had changed what I needed to do about the first.

## A month is not a container

Here is the mistake underneath all three.

We tend to think about a month as a box. Money comes in, money goes out, and if less goes out than came in, the month should work.

But the order matters too.

Your pay arrives on a particular date. Every bill has a date. Direct debits and standing orders have dates. Annual costs arrive in one month and not in the other eleven. Special offers and interest-free periods start and finish on particular dates.

That means you can have enough money over the month as a whole and still not have enough money on the day a payment is due.

The dates can move as well. Weekends and bank holidays can change when money is paid or received. A payment you expected on Monday might not arrive until Tuesday. A refund you were counting on might take longer than you expected. Pay can arrive earlier or later than you had planned for, depending on how your employer handles non-working days.

None of those changes needs to be large. It only needs to put money on the wrong side of a bill.

So after a bad month, the first question does not always have to be "what did I buy?" Sometimes the better question is "what came in and went out, and when?"

## The commitments nobody rechecks

The specific trap in my third story is worth looking at because it applies to more than credit cards.

Payment instructions keep running until something changes them. They don’t know that your circumstances have changed, and one payment arrangement does not know about another.

So when you change something, take an offer, switch provider, agree to a new payment plan or set up a new regular payment, check what you already have in place.

The new arrangement might be perfectly sensible while making an old one unsuitable.

That is what happened to me. The credit card did exactly what I had previously told it to do.

## Where this stops working

Sometimes the problem really is overspending. If the same thing happens month after month and there is nothing unusual about the timing, you may simply have more going out than you can afford.

Some months also cost more than others. Christmas, several birthdays close together, insurance and car tax arriving together, or an annual subscription can make one month much more expensive. Those are not accidents. They are costs we know about but can easily forget because they only appear once a year.

And knowing that the dates caused the problem does not help if there is no spare money in the first place. If every month is tight however the payments fall, changing the dates will not solve it.

Moving payment dates can help, but look ahead before changing anything. Fixing a problem this month is not much use if it creates another one next month.

## One thing to do

Write down the dates, not the amounts. It takes about ten minutes, and it is not something most of us have ever sat down and done.

Make one column for money coming in. Make another for money going out. Put every regular payment in it, with the day of the month beside it.

Then look for two things: payments that leave before your pay arrives, and weeks when several payments leave at once.

You are not making a budget. You are finding out when your money actually moves.

And that can explain a surprising number of months that seemed to go wrong for no reason.
