There is a coat hanging by my back door that I have not worn in months.

It is not a good coat. It is the waterproof one that goes on when the weather has already decided something, and for most of the year it does nothing at all except take up a hook. It has never once made me any money.

Then there is an afternoon when the sky opens while I am halfway down the garden, and it turns out that its entire job was to be there.

Money set aside works like that, and it is the reason people give up on it. It sits there. It earns almost nothing. Every month it looks like money that could have been doing something more interesting.

## Why it cannot stay where you spend from

Say you have found some money, and it really is spare.

Leave it in the account where your wages arrive and your everyday spending leaves, and it will vanish. Not dramatically. It will simply be absorbed, because that is where all your spending happens, and money sitting in the middle of your spending does not look like money set aside. It looks like a slightly better week.

That is not weakness and it is not a character flaw. It is what that account is for. The balance is a single number that answers one question, can I afford this today, and any money living inside that number will eventually be counted as a yes.

Now move it somewhere else.

Nothing has grown. Nothing is earning more. The money has not changed at all. But it has stopped being part of the answer to that question, and that turns out to be most of what you needed.

## Its first job is not to grow

Here is the part that takes longest to believe.

A reserve is not an investment that performs badly. It is a different kind of thing entirely, and what it gives you back is not more money.

Think about what happens without one. The washing machine dies. With money set aside, it is an annoying expense. Without it, the cost goes onto a card, and now every month for the next year or two has a little less room in it than it should. The machine cost the same either way. The borrowing is what made it expensive.

So what the money bought you was not interest. It was a dead washing machine staying a dead washing machine, instead of turning into a debt that follows you around for a year.

That is invisible on any statement, which is why it never feels as though the money has done anything.

The first version of this does not have to be large enough for a boiler. It may only be enough for the tyre, the prescription or the journey that would otherwise have gone onto the card. Its first job is to interrupt the pattern, not to solve every problem in it, and it can begin while older debt still exists.

Which is worth saying plainly, because the subject attracts a lot of quiet judgement. Some debt comes from spending too freely. A great deal of it comes from having no spare money in a month that asked for more than it contained.

## Where I actually am with this

I should be honest, since I have spent three articles insisting that honesty is the point.

I do not have as much set aside as I think I should. I know roughly what I want it to be, I know how I intend to get there, and I am not there yet. It is the least impressive part of my finances and it is the part I would fix first if I were starting again.

What I got wrong for years was the order. I found it much more interesting to send money forward into things that might grow than to build up something dull that just sat there. Growing money is a more enjoyable hobby than not borrowing money.

That preference cost me, in the years when something broke and the money had to come from somewhere less convenient.

## What this is called

Money you keep set aside so that an unexpected cost does not turn into borrowing is a cash reserve.

Some people call it an emergency fund. I prefer the duller name, because it makes the money feel available for a dead washing machine rather than only for a catastrophe.

That is the only term this article needs. Where to keep it, and what to look for in an account, is the next article.

## Where the analogy breaks

The coat by the door costs nothing to keep hanging there. Money does.

While it waits, it slowly buys less than it used to, because prices move and your balance does not. That is a real cost, and you are paying it in exchange for being able to reach the money as soon as something goes wrong.

A reserve is also not insurance. It is for the ordinary run of things breaking, not for a house fire or a long illness, and building one is not a reason to cancel cover that handles the genuinely large events.

It has a ceiling too. Once you have enough to absorb the ordinary disruptions, adding more to it stops doing much, because money that will not be needed for many years can be doing something else.

And do not confuse a reserve with saving up for something. Money for a holiday and money for the boiler have different jobs. One is for something you have chosen and can put in the diary. The other is for something you have not chosen and cannot. Put them in one account and you may find out which one you were really building on the day the boiler goes.

## One thing to do before the next article

Open somewhere separate. Not a spreadsheet, not a promise, not a mental note. A second account, at the same bank if that is easiest, taking about ten minutes on a phone.

Then give it a name that is a job rather than a category, because "boiler, car, dentist" is much harder to raid than "savings".

And if the money really is spare, tell your bank to move it there automatically on the day you are paid, so it leaves before you have started counting.

You have taken one number and stopped it answering the question "can I afford this today".
