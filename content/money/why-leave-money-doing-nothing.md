There’s a coat hanging by my front door that I haven’t worn in months.

It’s not a particularly good coat. It’s the waterproof one that goes on when the weather has already decided to tip it down, and for most of the year it does nothing at all except take up a hook. It has never once made me any money.

Then there’s an afternoon when the sky opens while I am halfway down the driveway, and it turns out that its entire job was to be there.

Money set aside works like that too, and it’s one reason people can find it difficult to keep any aside. It just sits there. It earns nothing, or almost nothing. Every month it looks like money that could’ve been doing something more interesting or useful.

## Why it can’t stay where you spend from

Say you have somehow found some extra money, and it really is spare.

Leaving it in the account where your wages arrive and your everyday spending leaves will probably mean it disappears, and often without any single decision to spend it. It will simply be absorbed, because that’s where all your spending happens. Money sitting in the middle of your ordinary spending doesn’t look like money set aside. It looks like a slightly better week, or month.

That’s not a weakness and it’s not a character flaw. It’s what that account is for. The balance is a single number that answers one question: can I afford this today? Any extra money living inside that number can eventually become part of the answer.

So move it somewhere separate.

The important part is the separation. Once the money is outside the account you use for everyday spending, it’s no longer sitting inside the balance you look at when deciding whether you can afford something.

The amount has not changed. What has changed is that spending it now requires a separate decision.

## Its first job isn’t to grow

Here’s the part that takes longest to believe.

A cash reserve isn’t an investment that performs badly. It has a different job.

Think about what happens when you do not have one. A washing machine dies. If you have money set aside, replacing it is an annoying expense. If you don’t, you may have to borrow the money, which means part of several future months’ income is already spoken for. If borrowing isn’t available, or would be too expensive, the other possibility is simply going without until you can find the money.

The washing machine has broken in every version. What changes is what happens next.

Money set aside can turn that event from a new debt, or weeks without a working machine, into an expense you can deal with and then move on from.

That benefit is easy to miss because there may be nothing obvious to show for it afterwards. The replacement machine’s there, the money’s gone, and your finances carry on. The thing the reserve prevented never appears in your normal everyday banking.

The first version of a reserve doesn’t have to be large enough for a boiler. It may only be enough for a tyre, a broken phone display or a necessary journey. The point is that the first unexpected bill doesn’t automatically have to go on a credit card, into an overdraft, or be postponed until you can somehow afford it.

It doesn’t solve every financial problem. It gives you another option when something goes wrong. And you can start building that option even while older debt still exists.

That’s worth saying plainly, because the subject attracts a lot of quiet judgement. Some debt comes from spending too freely. A great deal of it comes from having no spare money in a month that asked for more than was left.

## Where I actually am with this

I should be honest, since I have spent three articles insisting that honesty is the point.

I don’t have as much set aside as I think I should. I know roughly what I want it to be, I know how I intend to get there, and I’m not there yet. It’s the least impressive part of my finances and it’s the part I would fix first if I were starting again.

What I got wrong for years was the order. I found it much more interesting to send money forward into things that might grow than to build up something dull that just sat there.

That preference cost me over the years when something broke and the money had to come from somewhere that wasn’t designed to be a short-term solution.

## What this is called

Money you keep set aside so that an unexpected cost does not have to become borrowing is a cash reserve.

Some people call it an emergency fund. I prefer the duller name, because it makes the money feel available for a dead washing machine rather than only for a catastrophe.

That’s the only term this article needs. There are choices about where to keep the money, how quickly you need to be able to reach it and whether the interest rate matters. Those belong in the next article.

## Where the analogy breaks

The coat by the door costs nothing to keep hanging there. Money does.

While it waits, it slowly buys less than it used to, because prices rise while the number in the account may not. Interest can reduce that loss, and sometimes keep ahead of it, but leaving money readily available usually means accepting that its main job isn’t long-term growth.

That’s a real cost. You’re accepting it in return for having money available when something unexpected happens.

A reserve isn’t insurance. It’s for the ordinary run of things breaking and bills arriving at inconvenient times, not for a house fire or a long illness. Building one isn’t a reason to cancel cover for events that could cost far more than any sensible amount of cash sitting in an account.

It has a ceiling too. Once you have enough to absorb the ordinary disruptions, adding more starts to become less useful. Money that’s unlikely to be needed for many years can have a different job.

And don’t confuse a reserve with saving up for something. Money for a holiday and money for the boiler have different jobs. One is for something you have chosen and can put in the diary. The other is for something you haven’t chosen and can’t.

Put them together and the total may look reassuring, right up until the boiler breaks a week before the holiday.

## One thing to do before the next article

Open somewhere separate for the reserve. An easy-access savings account with the bank you already use may be enough, although a safe place you trust and can reach easily works too. Choosing between them, and deciding what matters in an account, is for the next article.

Give it a name that describes its job rather than simply calling it "savings". "Boiler, car, dentist" is harder to mistake for holiday money.

Then, if you’ve money that really is spare, arrange for some of it to move there automatically after you’re paid. Even a small amount begins building something you can use the next time an expense arrives that wasn’t in the plan.

You haven’t made the money grow.

You have given it a job.
