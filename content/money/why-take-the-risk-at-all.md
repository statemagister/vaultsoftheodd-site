Any food you can think of cost less when you were younger.

The food didn’t change all that much. A tin of beans is still a tin of beans. What changed was what the pound in your pocket could buy, and that can be hard to see from one month to the next and startling across thirty years.

The same thing happens to money sitting in a savings account. The number can go up whilst what it buys goes down.

## The safe option can lose too

Suppose you put £1,000 into a savings account paying 2% interest. At the end of the year you have £1,020.

Now suppose prices have risen by 4%. What cost £1,000 at the beginning of the year costs £1,040 at the end. Your savings have grown by £20, but the same things now cost £40 more. You have more pounds and can’t buy as much.

Nothing in your savings account tells you that. The balance has gone up, the interest has arrived, and there is no line on the statement saying that your money doesn’t go as far as it did.

Compare that with owning shares. If you bought £1,000 worth and they fell to £900, you would see the £100 loss immediately. You might worry about it, even if you had no intention of selling.

Both situations leave you worse off in some way, but only one puts a smaller number in front of you.

That’s easy to miss. A savings balance that never falls looks like money that can’t lose value.

## What owning can do differently

Most saving and investing comes down to two arrangements: lending, where somebody owes you under agreed terms, and owning, where you buy a piece of something and take whatever it becomes.

Here’s what owning can offer that a savings balance doesn’t.

When prices rise, businesses can put up their own prices too. If they can sell what they make for more without their costs rising just as much, their profits can grow. If you own shares in that business, you may benefit from that growth.

A business therefore has a chance to grow along with prices. Your savings account can only pay the interest offered by the bank, which may be enough to keep up or may not.

You don’t have to pick one business to do this. A fund can hold small pieces of hundreds or thousands of them, and some simply track a whole market, such as the UK, or several markets at once. It’s still owning. It’s just owning a great many businesses at the same time instead of one.

That’s the reason to consider owning something rather than keeping everything in cash. Not because shares always make more money, but because a business can grow in ways a savings balance cannot.

## My own version of it

Knowing what to buy has never been certain, and I’ve written about how I chose. Why to own shares at all was always clearer. Some of what I held paid me more in dividends than my savings paid in interest. For most of the last thirty years those dividends were reinvested, and they’ve grown.

I want to be careful with that, because it’s the point where a story like this over-claims. Dividends being larger than savings interest isn’t proof of keeping up with prices. A share can fall in value, dividends can be cut, and I have had one cut sharply. The comparison that matters is the whole return from the investment against the change in prices over years, and that’s a much less flattering calculation than the one that first excited me.

What I can say is narrower and still worth saying. Money left in savings would still have been there in pounds, but it wouldn’t have bought as much. Owning gave it a chance to do better. It’s worked so far, which isn’t the same thing as knowing it will continue to work.

## Which is why the date matters more than the nerve

The consequence of a fall depends partly on when you need the money.

Suppose you need the money next month and your investments have fallen. You cannot wait years for them to recover. You have to sell at whatever they’re worth, and do it early enough for the money to reach your bank in time.

But if you won’t need the money for another ten years, you may be able to leave your investments alone. The extra time gives them a chance to recover.

Over very long periods, history favours shares. Worldwide, since 1900, shares have grown by about 5% a year more than prices, while cash has barely kept ahead of them. Even the last twenty-five years, which include the dot-com crash and the financial crisis, ended with shares ahead of both prices and cash. That’s the record for markets as a whole, not for any one company, and it doesn’t mean every shorter period worked out.

At those long-run rates, £1,000 invested in shares for twenty years would grow to the equivalent of about £2,650 in today’s money. The same amount held in cash would grow to about £1,100. Those are averages, not a forecast.

That is why the answer to “should I invest this?” depends more on the date than on your appetite for risk or your nerve.

Money needed soon belongs somewhere you can reach it without having to sell an investment after a fall. Your savings might buy a little less over time, but you know how many pounds you have available.

Money you genuinely won’t need for years is the money investing is for. You have time to wait through a fall, provided you can afford to leave it alone.

## Where this stops working

**None of this means owning always wins.** Some businesses fail, and some shares never recover what you paid for them. Being able to wait doesn’t guarantee that waiting will help.

**And a fall can last for years.** It might be a long time before your investments are worth what they were, even if you own several different businesses. Keeping them doesn’t undo the loss. It only leaves open the possibility that their value recovers.

**Cash isn’t always the wrong choice.** If you need the money for rent, repairs, bills or something else you cannot postpone, having it available matters more than whether it keeps up with rising prices.

## One thing to do

Take one pot of money you have and answer one question about it: when do I need this?

Not what would I like to do with it. When is it needed, and how certain is that date?

If the answer is soon, unknown, or this is the money that stops a bad month becoming a debt, it needs to be somewhere you can reach without depending on a market price. It may buy less over time, but it will be there when you need it.

If the answer is genuinely years away and nothing else depends on it, keeping all of it in cash is a decision too. You avoid the worry of watching its value fall, but you may find it doesn’t go as far when the time comes to spend it.
