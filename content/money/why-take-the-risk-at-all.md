A tin of beans cost less when you were younger.

The beans did not have to change. What changed was what a pound would buy, and that can be hard to see from one month to the next and startling across thirty years.

That is the fact underneath this whole article. It is one reason accepting a visible fall can sometimes be more cautious than avoiding one.

## The safe option loses too

Prices rising do not make the number in a savings account fall. It may stay where it is, or rise with interest, and that feels like the absence of risk.

But that does not mean it stays the same size in terms of what it buys. If prices rise faster than the interest you are paid, the balance grows and the shopping it would cover shrinks. Nothing on the statement says so. There is no line that reads "this is now worth less than it was", and no month in which anything visibly goes wrong.

So there are two ways of losing money and only one of them announces itself.

One is a number that falls, which you can see, which feels awful, and which may recover. The other is a number that holds while everything around it moves, which is harder to see, and which does not repair the buying power already lost merely by holding still.

It was the loss I was choosing whenever I treated no visible fall as no risk.

## What owning can do differently

The previous article split everything into two arrangements: lending, where somebody owes you under agreed terms, and owning, where you buy a piece of something and take whatever it becomes.

Here is what the second can do that the first cannot.

Owning gives you an interest in a business whose prices, costs, products and profits can all change. That does not guarantee your share will keep up with rising prices. It means only that its future value is not fixed in pounds at the start. A cash balance grows only by whatever interest the account pays, and that may or may not keep pace with what those pounds will buy.

That is the trade. Not "risky things pay more because they are risky", which explains nothing. Owning something that can grow gives you a chance of keeping up. A cash balance does so only if the interest keeps pace.

The price of that chance is that you cannot know what it will be worth, and you do not get to choose when you find out.

## My own version of it

I started by buying shares in companies I already used, which I have written about, and I did it for reasons that were mostly not very good.

What changed my mind was noticing what the money was actually doing. Some of what I held paid me more in dividends than my savings paid in interest. Those dividends were not promised: they could grow, stop or be cut. That felt less like a clever trade and more like the money had a chance of staying level with the world instead of quietly slipping behind it.

I want to be careful with that, because it is the point where a story like this overclaims. Dividends being larger than savings interest is not proof of keeping up with prices. A share can fall in value, dividends can be cut, and I have had one cut sharply. The comparison that matters is the whole return from the investment against the change in prices over years, and that is a much less flattering calculation than the one that first excited me.

What I can say is narrower and still worth saying. The thing I had thought was the safe option was losing slowly and invisibly, and the thing I thought was reckless turned out to be the one with a chance of not doing that. I did not know that choice would work. It has worked so far, which is not the same thing.

## Which is why the date matters more than the nerve

The consequence of a fall depends partly on when you need the money.

Money you need next month cannot wait for a recovery, because you have to sell whatever it is worth on the day you need it. Money you will not touch for years may be able to wait. The fall is the same; the consequence changes with the date. The longer period does not guarantee recovery. It gives recovery time to happen.

That is why the answer to "should I invest this" is mostly not about your appetite for risk or your nerve. It is about the date. Money needed soon has no time to wait, so the possibility that prices outrun the interest is the price of being able to reach it. Money not needed for years has time that near-term money does not, which makes investing possible rather than compulsory.

Getting the date wrong creates one clear avoidable failure: being forced to sell after a fall.

## Where the analogy breaks

**None of this means owning always wins.** The argument is only that ownership has a chance to keep up. Some investments will not, and some will fail completely.

**And it can fall for a long time.** Not a bad week. Recovery can take years, and an individual investment may never recover at all.

**A fall is already a loss in value.** Selling makes it final in cash. Waiting helps only if the value later recovers and you can afford not to sell in the meantime, which is the whole point of matching money to dates.

**The quiet loss is not always the wrong choice either.** For money with a job soon, accepting the possibility that prices outrun the interest can be exactly the right call. This article is not an argument for putting everything at risk. It is an argument for knowing which loss you are choosing.

## One thing to do

Take one pot of money you have and answer one question about it: when do I need this?

Not what would I like to do with it. When is it needed, and how certain is that date.

If the answer is soon, unknown, or this is the money that stops a bad month becoming a debt, it needs to be somewhere you can reach without depending on a market price. The possibility that prices outrun the interest is the price of that certainty, and it may well be worth paying.

If the answer is genuinely years away and nothing else depends on it, keeping all of it in cash is a decision too. It trades visible movement for the possibility of a quieter loss.
