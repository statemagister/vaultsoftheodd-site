Skip one training session and nothing happens.

You get the time back immediately. The cost, if there is one, arrives much later and cannot be traced to the session you missed.

Skip enough of them and eventually you can see the difference. But there was never one session that made it.

A pension is like that, with even less feedback.

## The decision that reports nothing back

When you are put into a workplace pension automatically, you can choose to leave. You may already have done it, months or years ago. And the striking thing about opting out is how little happens afterwards.

Your pay changes because you stop contributing. Your pension changes too, because your own contribution stops and you may also lose money your employer was putting in. Tax can affect the difference as well. However it lands, you can see the change in what reaches your bank. What you cannot see is what the decision has taken away from later.

Nothing arrives to tell you what that is. No statement, no moment, no worse week you can point to. If you opted out years ago, nothing may have happened since to make you look at the decision again. Neither choice sends you a signal afterwards. Without one, it is easy for the decision to disappear from view. So it gets a shrug instead of a second thought.

That shrug is not a mistake of character. The problem is the question. *Will my future self really care?* asks you to know how somebody decades from now will feel. You cannot know that today.

## The question you can actually answer

So do not answer that one. Whether you are thinking of opting out or did it years ago, there is a question you can answer today: what am I giving up while I am out?

That is why the useful comparison is not a percentage or a rule. It is two numbers from your own scheme: what staying in costs you in take-home pay, and what reaches your pension when you stay in. Both are on your payslip or your pension's benefits page.

Put those two numbers beside each other. One tells you what staying in costs you today. The other tells you what is being built for later. You do not have to work out why they differ, or predict what the pension will be worth one day. You only have to see, in this month's actual figures, what being out costs you and what staying in would put away.

You can answer that today. You cannot know how your future self will feel.

## What this is called

Choosing to leave a pension you were automatically put into is called opting out. You can opt back in later.

The money your employer puts in is an employer contribution, and for eligible workers it is money you give up entirely by opting out. Pension contributions also receive tax relief. How that happens depends on the scheme: sometimes it changes the tax taken from your pay, sometimes the pension provider adds money claimed from HMRC. You do not need to work that out for this article.

## When stopping can make sense

What you missed while you were out does not come back, and money paid in earlier had longer to grow. Missing some time does not mean you have missed it for good.

And sometimes stopping is the right decision, in the same way skipping training is right when you are injured, because training through an injury is how you make it worse. If your month does not currently reach payday, money you can reach in an emergency may matter more urgently than money locked away for later, and opting out may be the sensible thing to do for exactly as long as that is true. The article that comes before the pension is [Why Leave Money Doing Nothing?](/money/why-leave-money-doing-nothing/), and it is the one to read first, because something you can reach in a bad week has to exist before something you cannot reach for thirty years. Opting out while you steady the present is not the mistake. Opting out and never looking again is where the cost hides.

If you are dealing with debt you cannot manage, the arithmetic can come out differently again, and that is a conversation for a [free debt adviser](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/money-troubles/dealing-with-debt/debt-advice-locator) rather than for an article.

Nothing here is about what your pension is invested in, or whether the default it came with is the right one. That is a separate decision, and it has [its own article](/money/who-chose-your-pension-investments/). This one is only about whether you are in at all.

## One thing to do

No arithmetic. Two numbers.

Find out two things: how much staying in changes what reaches your bank, and how much goes into your pension while you stay in. Both are on your most recent payslip or your pension information.

Put them next to each other. You do not have to calculate a return or imagine yourself at retirement. You only need to see what being out costs you and what staying in would put away.

Will your future self really care? You cannot ask them. But you can find out what being out gives you now and what staying in would put away for later. That is enough to make the decision with your eyes open, whether you are about to make it or made it long ago.
