There’s a light on upstairs in a room nobody’s using.

You notice it and you might turn it off, you almost certainly should.

You’d not ever ask yourself whether the light is useful, or whether you could manage without it. Nobody should want an empty room lit. The light’s still on because somebody switched it on earlier, then promptly forgot to turn it off again when they were finished.

Some types of payment work like that too.

You chose them once. They kept going. The service may actually still be useful. It may still be worth the money. But the company doesn’t need to ask whether you would choose it again today.

This article’s about asking.

## What I cancelled

I paid for Amazon Prime for years, mostly for next-day delivery.

Next-day delivery was useful. The real question was how often I’d actually needed it.

The answer was almost never.

It was pleasant not to wait, and convenience is a real thing to buy. But I’d been paying for that convenience for years, whether I needed it or not.

Then part of the service changed. I thought I was also paying to watch things without advertisements. Advertisements appeared anyway unless I chose to pay more.

I’d not just grown bored with the service. What I was buying had changed, and keeping the version I thought I already had now cost extra.

I also cancelled Now TV, Paramount and Disney.

By then, each had become a monthly payment for something I watched only a handful of times. I was paying every month for something I used a few times a year.

These services never look empty. There are always more programmes, more recommendations and more things saved for later.

I was not only paying for what I watched. I was paying for the comfort of knowing there would always be something there when I wanted it. A screen full of things I might watch is not the same as something I am actually choosing to watch.

## The one I kept, but stopped paying for

I paid for Duolingo for a long time, and I wasn’t sure I should.

I open it every day. I have a streak. I feel a small jolt when I nearly lose it, which keeps me coming back.

But I’d started to wonder whether I was learning a language or playing a game about learning one.

The problem was that I never decided what result I was paying for.

If I was paying to hold a conversation, I should’ve been able to tell whether I was getting closer to one.

If I was paying for an enjoyable ten minutes and a number that goes up, that might be fine too. But it’s a different thing to buy, and it may be worth a different amount.

I’ve stopped paying for it. Not stopped using it. I still open it every day, I’ve just gone back to the free version, because that was giving me the ten minutes and the streak anyway. The paid part was the bit that couldn’t answer what it was for.

## The test

The first question I asked was:

What can I manage without?

That makes cancellation sound like deprivation. It turns the decision into a test of how much you are willing to give up.

A better question is:

If it weren’t already running, would I start it today, at what it costs now?

That’s the only test in this article.

It’s not asking whether the service is good. It’s not asking whether you could survive without it.

It’s asking whether you would choose to buy another month or another year, knowing how much you’d use it and what it now provides.

Several of my subscriptions failed that test.

That wasn’t because I suddenly became more disciplined. I’d made the original decision before I knew how much I would use the service. The renewal kept using that old decision long after I’d stopped thinking about it.

## What it was costing me

Here’s the part I am least comfortable writing down.

I can’t tell you what Amazon Prime cost when I cancelled it.

I can’t tell you the price of Now TV, Paramount or Disney either.

I could look through old statements and find the figures, but that would make the story tidier than it really was.

I cancelled four services and could not have told you the price of any of them to within a couple of pounds.

That shows how little attention they were receiving from the person paying for them.

Duolingo is different because I checked.

When I looked, the family plan cost £89.99 a year. That’s about £7.50 a month.

The family plan covers several people, so the decision wasn’t mine alone. The question was whether we would choose that plan again today, at what it now costs, for what all of us are getting from it.

£7.50 a month can matter when money is tight.

On a statement, it can also look small enough to pass without much thought. No single payment felt large enough to make me stop everything and examine it.

Several of them together would have.

Each one was charged again and again until I actively stopped it. If the total had been requested for a whole year in one payment, I would probably have thought about it much sooner.

## What this is called

When you agree to keep paying for access to something until you stop, that’s a subscription.

When the agreement carries on into another month or another year unless you intervene, that is an automatic renewal.

Nobody asks you to make the decision again because the agreement you already made allows the company to keep charging you.

The money may leave through a payment card or directly from your bank account. Those methods have different names and different rules, which matter when something goes wrong.

That’s not the issue here.

The question here is whether you still want what you agreed to keep paying for.

## Where the analogy breaks

In the opening example, the light is serving no useful purpose. Nobody needs the room lit.

A subscription may still be doing something useful even when you rarely notice it.

Insurance you never claim on has not necessarily failed. It may be protecting you from a cost you couldn’t manage alone. Cancelling it because nothing has happened can leave you borrowing when something does.

Something used four times a year can also be worth every penny. A service you use rarely and love is different from one you use rarely and have forgotten about.

Convenience is a real purchase too.

Someone with young children, limited mobility or an awkward shift pattern may be paying to avoid a difficult journey or save time they badly need. Using a service less often does not automatically make it poor value.

Cancelling can sometimes cost more than it saves. You may lose an old price, or removing one service from a bundle may make the others more expensive.

These aren’t reasons to leave every payment running.

They’re reasons to decide carefully rather than assume that every recurring payment is waste.

## One thing to do before the next article

Take one month of your bank statement.

One month. Not the whole year, not a spreadsheet and not a new project.

Go through it and mark every recurring payment that you’d not start today at what it now costs.

Don’t cancel everything at once. Pick the clearest one.

Before stopping it, check whether anyone else uses it and whether leaving would lose a price or bundle you still want.

Then see what stopping the payment actually changes.

Perhaps it means that less of this month has to be borrowed. That’s already useful. The payment hasn’t created spare money, but it has stopped the gap getting wider.

Perhaps the money really is free. Decide where it will go before it quietly disappears into the rest of the month.

It could go towards a debt, or towards money kept for an emergency. It could pay for something that helps you work or earn. Or it could go towards something that may pay you later.

Stopping a payment does not always create spare money.

Sometimes it only means that you finish the month less far behind.
