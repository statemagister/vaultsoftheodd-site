Before men made laws about truth, there was Math.

He did not decide what was true. He was not a judge sitting above it, weighing one man's word against another's.

Where Math was, what was true could not be made otherwise.

You could hide something from him. You could keep your mouth shut. Gwydion did both, and more than once.

But a lie was another thing.

---

He kept a house, and it was not an ordinary one.

His nephew Gwydion lived in it, and Gwydion was the cleverest person anyone had ever met. He could make twelve horses out of toadstools and put gold on their bridles. He could make a ship out of seaweed and a fleet out of nothing at all. He could walk into a room wearing another man's face and walk out again with what he came for.

His niece Aranrhod lived in it too, and strange things happened around her, and more will be said of that.

And Math sat at the centre of them.

Gwydion could show the world what was not there.

Math could show it what was.

Between those two things lay almost every trouble that ever came to that house.

---

There was a young woman in it called Goewin, daughter of Pebin, and she was in Math's keeping.

That is worth stopping on, because everything turns on it. To be in the keeping of Truth is not like being under a roof or behind a wall. What was given into Math's care was not lightly taken out of it, and everybody knew so, and that was supposed to be enough.

Gilfaethwy was Gwydion's brother, and Gilfaethwy wanted her.

He wanted her until he stopped eating. He wanted her until the colour went out of him and his own family walked past him without knowing him. And one day Gwydion looked at his brother properly and said, what has happened to you?

Gilfaethwy would not say.

"Tell me."

"I cannot tell you."

"Why?"

Gilfaethwy looked at the door, and at the window, and at the air.

"Because Math will hear."

Gwydion looked at him for a long moment.

"You love Goewin."

He said her name out loud. In that house. In that hall.

Both brothers waited.

Nothing happened.

---

Gwydion was not a fool, and he had just learned something, though it would be a while before he understood how much.

What he wanted now was his uncle out of the house.

So he went and stood in front of Math and told him about pigs.

There are beasts in the south, he said, that never came to this island before. Their flesh is sweeter than beef. They came out of Annwn, out of the country under the world, and they were given to Pryderi son of Pwyll. I will go and ask him for them.

Math said, he may refuse you.

"I have a trick," said Gwydion.

Math looked at him.

"And I will not come back without the pigs."

He did not tell Math what the trick was.

Math did not ask.

---

Pryderi kept a court at Rhuddlan Teifi, and twelve poets came to it out of the north, and the best storyteller in the world was among them.

Gwydion held that hall all evening. He told them tales they had never heard, and made them laugh, and made them quiet, and by the time the fire was low Pryderi would have given him almost anything.

Not the pigs, though. Pryderi had promised his people the pigs would stay until they had bred to twice their number, and a promise is a promise.

Then I will not ask you to break it, said Gwydion. Do not give them to me. Do not refuse me either. Tomorrow I will show you a trade.

That night he went out to the wood.

He gathered toadstools, and out of them he made twelve stallions, black and bright, with saddles on them and bridles on them, and every buckle and every ring that should have been iron was gold. Out of toadstools he made twelve hounds, white-chested, with collars and leashes. Out of toadstools he made twelve golden shields.

In the morning he walked all of it into the hall, and it shone, and Pryderi's council said, take the trade.

Pryderi took the trade.

Then Gwydion ran.

Move, he told his men, we go now and we go fast. The making will not hold past this hour tomorrow.

They drove the pigs north through the whole night. Behind them a man slept in Ceredigion believing himself twelve horses and twelve hounds and twelve golden shields the richer, and the morning came up over a stable full of mushrooms.

---

Pryderi came after them with an army, which was exactly what Gwydion had wanted.

Math went to meet it. A war had come north, and Math went with the host.

While he was gone, Gwydion and Gilfaethwy turned their horses round and rode back to the house.

They put the other women out of the room. Gilfaethwy took Goewin that night, in Math's own chamber, against her will, and in the morning the two of them rode back to the army as though they had never been away.

---

The war ended badly for everyone. Pryderi asked that it be settled between himself and Gwydion alone rather than have more men die over pigs, and Gwydion killed him, and they buried him above the ford, and the men of the south went home carrying a grief they had not earned.

Math came home too.

Goewin was waiting for him, and she did not let him take a step further into the room.

She told him. She did not soften it and she did not leave anything out. Your nephews came. Your sister's sons. They shamed you and they shamed me, and it was done here, in your own house, in your own bed.

Math listened.

He did not question her.

"Your redress first," he said. "Mine afterwards."

Then he stood.

---

The brothers ran.

For a while it worked. Then the country closed around them. There was no house in Gwynedd where they were given food, and no hall where they were given a bed, and no fire anyone would let them sit at. They went out and came back and went out again, and in the end there was nowhere left in the whole of the north to be, except the one hall they had run from.

They walked back into it.

Have you come to make amends, Math said.

We are at your will.

He did not argue with them. He did not ask them to explain. He did not tell them what they had done, because they knew what they had done, and so did he, and saying it would have changed nothing.

He took up the rod.

"You made another body answer to your appetite," said Math. "Now yours will answer."

He touched Gilfaethwy with it, and Gilfaethwy was a hind. Gwydion turned to run and the rod found him too, and he was a stag.

"You are bound together. Go together. When these creatures have young, so will you. Come back to me in a year."

---

They came back in a year.

There was a noise at the wall in the dark and the dogs of the court went wild at it, and when Math came out there was a stag and a hind, and standing between them on new legs was a fawn.

Math looked at the fawn.

The world had gone on from what he had made.

He raised the rod. The one of you who was a hind will be a boar. The one who was a stag will be a sow. And he struck them.

The boy I will keep, he said. They washed him and named him Hyddwn, and he grew up in that house.

A year later there was a boar and a sow at the wall, and a young pig with them. The rod came down on the young pig and there was a boy standing there instead, auburn-haired, and they called him Hychdwn. The rod came down on the parents and there was a wolf and a she-wolf.

A year later there was a wolf and a she-wolf and a strong cub, and the cub was made a boy, and they called him Bleiddwn.

Three years. Three shapes. Three children, and not one of them went away when the magic stopped. They were baptised. They were given names. They grew up as men, and people who met them thought nothing of it, because there was nothing to think.

Then Math struck his nephews one last time, and two men stood in his hall in their own flesh.

He looked at them for a while.

"You have paid. Go and wash. Get dressed."

Peace they had had. Kinship they would have again.

---

There is one more thing to tell about the rod, and it happened later.

Math's niece Aranrhod came into the hall, because Gwydion had said her name when Math asked who might come into his house.

"Are you a maiden?" Math asked.

"As far as I know," said Aranrhod.

Math said nothing at all.

He took the rod and laid it across the floor in front of her.

"Step over it."

Aranrhod stepped.

And a child fell from her onto the floor of the hall and cried out, and she ran for the door, and something else dropped from her as she went that Gwydion caught up into a fold of silk before anybody had properly looked at it.

Math never called her a liar. He never had to. He had asked the world instead, and the world had answered, the way it always did when he was standing there.

---

The child Gwydion caught became a boy, and the boy became Lleu.

Years afterwards Lleu was speared by a man who wanted his wife, and he did not die; he went up out of his own shape as an eagle and hung in an oak tree in a valley nobody knew, rotting, while the flesh dropped off him.

Gwydion looked for him for a year. He found him at last by following a sow, and he sang him down out of that tree one branch at a time, and what came down onto his knee was bones and skin and hardly anything else.

Gwydion had the rod with him.

He struck.

And there was a man. Not the look of a man. A man, ruined, breathing, a year away from being able to stand, and alive.

That rod had been in Math's hand seven times and in Gwydion's once, and the once made no difference to it. It never made anything seem. It only ever made things be.

---

So. What Math did.

He made the hidden known. He made the brothers carry what they had done until the reckoning was complete. He gave them their shapes back, and peace and kinship followed.

Goewin had spoken, and her redress had come before his.

And Goewin remembered.

Because what had happened was true.

Math could change what stood in front of him. He could make the hidden known. He could change flesh and shape, and what he changed could live and breed and remain after the rod was lowered.

But what had happened to Goewin belonged to Truth too.

To make it never have happened would not have been a greater act of Math's power. It would have been a lie.

And Math was Truth.

---

## A note on this story

This is a retelling, but not only a retelling.

Math son of Mathonwy comes down to us in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, a medieval Welsh tale, where he appears as a lord of Gwynedd who happens to be a magician. Most of the events above are in that tale: the pigs, the toadstool gold, the war, Goewin, the three years of beasts, Aranrhod and the rod, the eagle in the oak.

What is different here is the shape of the person at the centre of them. Read closely, the surviving story is strange about Math in ways it never explains. Nobody in it succeeds in lying to him. His magic never once makes a false appearance, though the magic all around him does constantly. What he changes stays changed, and breeds, and leaves children behind it.

The suggestion of the longer work behind this story is that a god has been remembered here as a king, and that the name of what he was is Truth. The medieval tale also gives Math a peculiar condition, that he could not live unless his feet were held in a maiden's lap except in wartime. That has been left out, deliberately, because nothing about it follows from what he seems to be, and there is no good reason to build a god out of the chains a story put on him.

If you want the evidence, it is set out in full elsewhere, with the counting, the objections, and an account of the places where the argument was tested and failed.
