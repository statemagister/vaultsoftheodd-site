subtitle: "The reconstruction, told through the text"

description: "Recovering Math as the god of Truth: the reconstruction read through the Fourth Branch, with the counting, the speech-distribution and the rod that never lies."
---

Math is the god of Truth. Not honesty, which is a human virtue and much smaller. Not a detector of lies, which would make him an instrument rather than a power.

Reality cannot lie to him, and he cannot make reality lie.

In the oldest story that remains of him, he sits at Caer Dathyl with his feet in the lap of a young woman.

---

The story calls him lord of Gwynedd. It gives him a sister, two nephews, a niece, a household and a realm. That is the shape in which they have all reached us: a family arranged like a medieval dynasty, ruling from a hall.

The family does not behave like one.

Human speech reaches the lord of it on the wind. When he takes up his rod, flesh and sex and species stop being fixed things, and what he makes of a man will breed true and leave living children behind it. One nephew builds false worlds out of fungus and seaweed and can tell you the hour they will end. The niece's body is caught up in impossible births, and in the giving and withholding of names and arms and destinies.

There are magicians in these stories, and Gwydion is one of them; you are about to watch him work, and he is worth watching. But the question this house raises is not why a king's nephew should also be a wizard.

It is what kind of family has been remembered here as a royal house.

The kingdom comes to Math, and his nephews ride its circuit in his place, and he sits.

He sits because the tale places one extraordinary restriction on him. Unless there is war, Math cannot live unless his feet are in the lap of a virgin.

It never says why. No cause, no origin, no story behind it. It is simply true of him from the first page.

I am going to leave that where the story put it. Perhaps something older survives in it. Perhaps the tale has bound an older power to the machinery of its own plot, since a being who cannot leave his hall except in wartime is exactly what this particular plot requires. I do not know, and I am not going to build a god out of his chains.

But it does mean everything turns on a young woman's lap. She is Goewin, daughter of Pebin of Dol Pebin, the fairest anyone knew of at that time. Hold onto her. Almost everything that follows happens because of where she sits.

And there is one more thing they say about him, which is the reason people in that court are careful what they say in it.

Gilfaethwy has begun to waste. His colour goes, and his shape, and his bearing, until he is hard to recognise, and one day his brother looks at him properly and asks what has happened to him.

Gilfaethwy will not say. Not from shame. Because of where they are standing.

You know Math's nature, he says. Whatever whisper passes between people, however quietly spoken, if the wind gets hold of it, he will know it.[^1]

And Gwydion says: be quiet, then. I know your mind. You love Goewin.

He says it aloud, in his uncle's court, four lines after being warned.

Nothing happens.

---

Do not sigh over it, Gwydion tells his brother. Nothing is got that way. I will bring it about.

Only one thing takes Math out of that chair. War. So there will have to be a war, and to have a war somebody in the south will need a reason, and Gwydion goes to his uncle with an idea about pigs.

Beasts have come to the south, he says, such as never came to this island before. They are called *hobeu*. Their flesh is better than beef. They belong to Pryderi, and they were sent to him out of Annwn, from Arawn who is king there. I will go as one of twelve in the guise of poets, and ask for them.

Math says Pryderi may well refuse.

My device is not a bad one, lord, says Gwydion. I will not come back without the pigs.[^3]

Math lets him go.

---

Pryderi keeps court at Rhuddlan Teifi, and twelve poets arrive there, and the best storyteller in the world is among them.

That night Gwydion holds the hall. He tells tales and makes talk until everyone in the place is delighted with him and Pryderi is delighted to be sitting beside him, and only at the end of the evening, with the room warm to him, does he ask for the swine.

He cannot have them. Pryderi has given his country his word: the pigs stay until they have bred to twice their number.

Then let me free you from those words, says Gwydion. Do not give them to me tonight. Do not refuse them either. Tomorrow I will show you an exchange.

That night he goes out to his craft.

Out of toadstools he makes twelve stallions, and on every one of them a saddle and a bridle, and every part that should have been iron is gold. Out of toadstools he makes twelve greyhounds, white-breasted, black, with collars and leashes on them. Out of toadstools he makes twelve shields of gold.

In the morning he brings the whole shining lot of it into Pryderi's court and offers it for the swine. Pryderi's council tells him to take the exchange. He takes it.

Then Gwydion runs.

Come on, he tells his men, we have to move fast. The enchantment will not last past this hour into the same hour tomorrow.[^2]

They drive the pigs north all night, stopping at places that are called Pigtown to this day because of it. Behind them, in a hall in Ceredigion, a man is asleep believing himself twelve horses and twelve hounds and twelve golden shields the richer, and the sun is coming up on a stableful of mushrooms.

---

Gwydion is very good at making one thing pass for another.

He will do it again before the story is finished. He will come to his sister's fortress in the shape of a shoemaker to trick a name out of her. He will come back in the shape of a bard from Morgannwg. He will fill the bay with a fleet that is not there and then bring her the news of it, so that she arms with her own hands the boy she has sworn will never carry arms.

He lies to whoever is standing in front of him, fluently, and it works.

---

Yet when Gwydion needs something from Math, the lies stop.

Go back and look at what he actually said about the pigs. Beasts have come to the south such as never came here: true. They are called *hobeu*: true. Their flesh is better than beef: true. They are Pryderi's, sent out of Annwn by Arawn: true. I will go as one of twelve in the guise of poets and ask for them: true, and precisely what he does.

Every word of it is so. And it is a lever.

What he does not say is that he means to take them by a trick, or that the war is not a risk of the errand but the point of it, or that the reason he wants his uncle up out of that chair is so that his brother can get to the girl who holds his feet.

And now the one line I have not accounted for. My device is not a bad one.

The word is *trawscwyd*, and we do not have to guess at it, because the story uses it again thirty lines later. When the twelve poets ask how the swine are to be got, since asking will not do it, they ask by what *trawscwyd* they shall be had. A device. A stratagem. A trick.

So Gwydion does not slip past his uncle with a technically true sentence about livestock. He stands in front of him and tells him, in the plain word for it, that he has a trick.

And Math lets him go without asking what it is.

---

The war goes as wars do. Fighting at Maenor Bennardd and Maenor Coet Alun, a retreat, hostages, a truce that does not hold. In the end Pryderi asks that it be settled between himself and Gwydion alone, body against body, rather than have more men die for a quarrel about pigs. Math agrees. Gwydion kills him by strength and by magic together. They bury him at Maen Tyfiawg and the men of the south go home carrying a great sorrow.

While that is happening, two men leave the army and ride back to Caer Dathyl.

They put Goewin out of Math's bed and drive the other maidens out of the room, and Gilfaethwy sleeps with her that night against her will, in Math's chamber, in Math's bed.

Then they rejoin the host as though nothing had happened.

---

The men of Gwynedd come home in high spirits. Math comes to Caer Dathyl and has his chamber made ready and goes to put his feet in Goewin's lap.

She stops him.

Lord, she says, seek a maiden to hold your feet now. I am a woman.

He asks her what she means. And she tells him, without softening any of it: they came to me, and it was not done quietly enough for me to keep quiet, and I did not keep quiet. Your nephews came. Your sister's sons, Gwydion and Gilfaethwy. They raped me, and they shamed you, and it was done in your own chamber and in your own bed.[^4]

He answers her at once. I will do what I can. I will get redress for you first and then I will get it for myself. And I will take you as my wife and put the authority of my realm into your hands.

Then he goes looking for his nephews.

---

They run. For a while it works.

Then the country closes. Food is refused them, and drink after it, wherever they go. There is no house left in Gwynedd where the sons of Dôn can eat, and no hall where they can sleep, and in the end there is nowhere to be but Caer Dathyl, and they turn round and walk back into it.

He asks whether they have come to make amends. They say they are at his will.

If it were my will, he says, I would not have lost the men and the arms that I lost. You cannot pay me for my shame, let alone for Pryderi's death.

He does not argue with them. He reaches for the rod.

He strikes Gilfaethwy, and Gilfaethwy is a hind. Gwydion turns to run and cannot, and the same rod takes him, and he is a stag. Since you are bound together, says Math, you will go together and mate together and have the nature of the beasts whose shape you wear, and when they have young, so will you. A year from today, come back here.

A year later there is a noise at the wall, and the dogs of the court are baying at it, and Math goes out and finds a stag and a hind and a strong fawn standing in the dark.

He raises the rod. The one of you that was a hind last year will be a wild boar this year. The one that was a stag will be a wild sow. And he strikes them.

The boy I will take, he says, and have him reared and baptised. Hyddwn, they call him.

Another year. Another noise at the wall. A boar and a sow and a sturdy young pig. He strikes the young pig and there is a fine auburn-haired boy standing where it was, and they name him Hychdwn. He strikes the parents and they are a wolf and a she-wolf.

Another year. A wolf, a she-wolf, and a strong cub. The cub becomes a boy and they call him Bleiddwn. Three sons in three years, and the story stops long enough to give the verse about them: the three sons of false Gilfaethwy, three true champions, Bleiddwn, Hyddwn, Hychdwn the Tall.

Then he strikes them both one last time, and there are two men standing in his hall in their own flesh.

Men, he says, you have made amends to me. Take baths. Have your heads washed. Get dressed.

And then he forgives them.

They did not look like deer for a year. They were deer.

And nature took them at their word. The stag and the hind could breed because they were a stag and a hind. When he made them swine they bred as swine, and when he made them wolves they bred as wolves, and each time there was a living child at the wall a year later.

The world does not correct Math. It continues from what he has made.

The children do not vanish when the magic ends. Math takes them in. They are baptised. They are given names. They grow up as men.

---

But he still has nobody to hold his feet, and he asks his nephews which maiden he should seek.

Easily advised, says Gwydion. Aranrhod. Our sister. Your niece.

She comes into the hall.

Are you a maiden, Math asks her.

I know of nothing other than that I am.

He does not argue with her. He takes the rod and bends it.

Step over this, he says.

She steps.

And a large fine boy falls from her onto the floor of the hall and cries out, and she runs for the door, and on the way something else drops from her that Gwydion snatches into a sheet of silk before anyone has looked at it twice. The first is Dylan, who goes into the sea and swims as well as the best fish in it. The second, in time, is Lleu.[^5]

Math never calls her a liar. He has no need to. He asks reality instead, and reality answers.

Her answer had lasted three lines.

It is the only time in the whole of the Fourth Branch that anyone tells Math something false. Sixteen things are said to him across the surviving text. Fifteen are true. Aranrhod's is the sixteenth.[^6]

Math does not argue with the lie. He bends the rod.

---

That rod is worth following.

Math's rod does not make the world look different. It makes the world different.

It turns up ten times in the branch, and no other word for a rod or a staff turns up anywhere in it at all. Twice it is only lifted or bent. The other eight times somebody strikes with it.[^7]

Seven of those eight are Math.

The eighth is at the very end. Lleu has been speared by his wife's lover, and has gone up out of his own body in the shape of an eagle, and has hung in an oak in Nantlleu rotting while the flesh drops off him, and Gwydion has followed a sow to find him and sung him down out of the tree branch by branch. What comes down onto Gwydion's knee is skin and bone and almost nothing.

Gwydion takes the rod and strikes him.

And there is a man. Not the look of a man. A man, so wasted that nobody ever saw a worse sight, and alive, and a year in the healing.

Seven times the rod is in Math's hand. The eighth time it is in another magician's, and it still refuses to make a seeming.

---

Set that beside the rest of the magic in the same story. Gold that was fungus and had a clock running on it. A fleet that was never in the bay. A shoemaker who was no shoemaker and a bard who was no bard. Blodeuwedd, made out of oak flowers and broom and meadowsweet, who might look like the exception and is not, because she is no illusion at all: she is a woman, she goes on being one, and the ruin she causes is entirely real.

Gwydion can make a thing seem what it is not. That is his whole method, and it is why he is so effective and so destructive. Math has the same reach into what things are, and never once uses it to make one thing pass for another.

But the deeper difference is not in what they can do. It is in what they have to do.

Gwydion needs a story, a disguise, a fungus, a fleet, a stolen name, an hour's head start. He devises, he performs, he persuades, he runs. Everything he achieves he has to arrange.

Math reaches for the rod.

He strikes.

Gwydion does things. Math is something.

Which is the sentence from the beginning, arriving from underneath instead of from above.

Reality cannot lie to Math, and Math cannot make reality lie.

Or, since we are past being careful about it: reality cannot lie across Math, and what Math makes, is.

---

And the Fourth Branch is not the only place the tradition remembered him.

Welsh storytellers kept his name outside this tale altogether. He stands in the Triads among the great enchanters of the Island of Britain. He surfaces in the Book of Taliesin, and among the later poets, who reach for him when they want a word for enchantment itself. Whoever wrote the branch down was not inventing a magician for the occasion; he was writing about somebody his audience already knew of, and knew of in that register.[^10]

Which is worth holding beside the man in the chair. The story we have gives us a lord of Gwynedd who cannot cross his own country. The tradition around the story remembers something the whole island had a word for.

And if this figure has come down to us reduced to a king, there is no particular reason to assume the genealogy around him came through unchanged. What survives may not be one impossible magician among ordinary relatives. It may be the relationships of an older company, remembered in the only language a medieval hall had for such things, which is kinship and lordship.

I am not going to claim that here. Gwydion, Aranrhod and the rest have not had done to them what has been done to Math in the paper behind this article, and each of them will have to be recovered on their own evidence or not at all. But it is worth saying what the shape of the question is. Not why is this king's nephew a wizard. What was this household, before it was a household.

---

There is still the scene we left hanging.

Gilfaethwy would not speak because Math would hear. Gwydion spoke anyway and nothing came of it, and that has bothered readers for a long time. Catherine McKenna reads it as part of the tale's joke about how little Math knows: he is ignorant of his nephews' designs, of how the pigs were got, of Pryderi's fury, of the invasion until it is nearly on top of him. Ian Hughes, editing the same lines, stops one step short. He notes that Gwydion appears not to fear the consequences of speaking aloud. He does not say that Math failed to hear him.[^8]

We do not know. We do not need to know.

Because not one of the things Math fails to know is a lie told to his face. They are plans kept behind teeth, tricks worked on other men in other kingdoms, an army still a day off. When Lleu falls, the story does not say the wind brought it to Caer Dathyl. It says the news reached him.[^9]

Silence is not falsehood. A trick you decline to describe is not a lie. Truth does not extract, and it does not compel confession; that is not a gap in it, it is what it is. A man may stand in front of Truth, tell it nothing but the truth, say plainly that he is holding something back, and walk out of the hall with permission. That is how a god gets robbed of his swine, dragged into somebody else's war, and violated in his own bedroom by two young men who never told him a single untruth.

Gwydion stood in front of him and said, I have a device. And walked out with permission.

---

Which brings it back to Goewin, where it started.

Gwydion got past Truth by keeping quiet. Goewin got through to Truth by speaking. She says what happened; it reaches him; it is acted on before the hour is out. That is the whole shape of him, in two people.

And then there is the thing he cannot do.

He can expose what was hidden. He can hear her. He can judge it, and punish it, and turn the guilty into beasts and back again, and marry her, and put his realm in her hands, and alter everything that comes afterwards.

Truth can change what is. It cannot change what was into what was not.

For another magician that would be the place where his power failed. For Math, power is not the question. What happened to Goewin is true. To make it never have happened would be to make a false world, and it would take Truth to do it.

That is not something Math fails to do. It is something Math cannot do and remain Math.

Goewin is not mentioned again.

---

There are three different claims in what I have just told you, and they are not equally strong.

Some of it you can count. Sixteen statements made to him. Ten appearances of the rod. Eight strokes. One lie, and it fails.

Some of it is my argument: that these things belong together, and that Truth is what joins them.

And one step is larger than either. The counts can show the pattern. They cannot tell us what Math was before this story made him a king in a hall. My reconstruction is that the answer is a god, and that the name of his power was Truth.

Every number here comes from a sweep of Ian Hughes's critical edition, and the line references in the notes are to it. Sioned Davies's translation in the Oxford *Mabinogion* is the best way to read the story itself.

If you would rather test this than take it, there is a quick way. Read the branch and look for someone lying to Math and getting away with it.

There are six hundred and sixty lines. I could not find one.

[^1]: Lines 25 to 27 of Hughes's edition. Hughes glosses *kynedyf* as a magical peculiarity or characteristic, setting it beside the cauldron of the Second Branch and the special attributes of Cei in Culhwch. The tradition's closest parallel is the Coraniaid of Cyfranc Lludd a Llefelys, who likewise hear whatever is spoken if the wind meets it. The branch states the characteristic here and nowhere else.

[^2]: Lines 91 and 96 to 97: the horses and hounds were conjured from toadstools, and Gwydion says the enchantment will not last beyond this hour to the same.

[^3]: Line 46: *Nit drwc uyn trawscwyd i, Arglwyd*. The controlling sense comes from the branch itself: at line 75 the same word is used of the means by which the swine are to be obtained once asking has failed, *pa drawscwyd y keir wynteu*, by what device shall they be got. Both manuscripts agree at both places.

[^4]: Lines 181 to 188. Her phrasing is exact and worth having in the original sense: they raped me, and they shamed you.

[^5]: The question and answer at line 263, the rod at 264 to 266. The verb Math uses of the rod and the verb Aranrhod uses of herself are the same word, three lines apart: he bends it, she bends herself over it.

[^6]: At lines 36 to 38, 39, 39 to 43, 44 to 45, 46 to 47, 118 to 120, 152 to 155, 171 to 173, 181 to 188, 196 to 197, 260 to 261, 263, 419 to 420, 430 to 431, 546 to 547 and 602 to 605. The full inventory is in the working audit accompanying the paper.

[^7]: Lines 203, 205, 218, 220, 234, 237, 251, 264, 266 and 597. Those at 218 and 264 are the raising and the bending. The eighth stroke, at 597, is Gwydion's.

[^8]: McKenna, "Revising Math: Kingship in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi," Cambrian Medieval Celtic Studies 46 (2003), 95 to 117, at 114 and note 65. Hughes, note to line 25.

[^9]: Line 543. Every instance of Math's knowledge in the branch comes to him by report or by enacted test.

[^10]: The references outside the branch are gathered by Hughes and by Rachel Bromwich; see *Trioedd Ynys Prydein*, 2nd edn (Cardiff, 1978), pp. 448 to 450, and Triad 28 at p. 56. One caution belongs with this. The White Book text of Triad 28 says that Math taught his magic to Gwydion, and Bromwich takes that particular detail to be derived from the Fourth Branch itself rather than independent of it. What the wider tradition does establish, in the Triads, the Book of Taliesin and the poetry of the *cywyddwyr*, is that Math was known as a magician well beyond this one tale.
